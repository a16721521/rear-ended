Amusement park & venue injuries

When a ride fails or security fails, the operator's records tell the story.

Amusement parks, concert venues, sports stadiums, fairs, and carnivals owe every visitor a duty of reasonable care under Civil Code § 1714. For fixed amusement rides, that duty is reinforced by a specific state inspection regime: California's Permanent Amusement Ride Safety Inspection Program, codified at Labor Code § 7920 et seq., requires Cal/OSHA's Amusement Ride and Tramway Unit to inspect and permit fixed rides annually. The operator's own daily pre-opening inspection logs, maintenance and repair records, and any incident reports filed with the state after a prior malfunction are often the most important evidence in a ride injury case — they show whether the ride was maintained to the standard the permit required.

Ride and venue injuries come from several recurring sources: mechanical or ride malfunctions such as restraint systems that fail to lock or brakes that don't engage; improper restraint use or negligent operator training that lets a ride run with a guest improperly secured; inadequate crowd control during loading, unloading, or emergency evacuation; slip and fall hazards in queue lines, walkways, and common areas; and food or concession negligence, including undercooked food and unsanitary preparation. Each requires a different evidentiary strategy, but all trace back to the same duty — the operator controls the premises and the equipment, and the operator controls what gets maintained.

Large operators routinely raise an assumption of risk defense, arguing that speed, sudden drops, or crowded venues are inherent to the experience you paid for. California courts allow that defense only for the risks genuinely inherent to the activity — it does not protect an operator from claims based on negligence that increases the risk beyond what a reasonable guest expects, such as a defective restraint, deferred maintenance, or a ride operated outside its permit conditions. Waivers and release language printed on tickets and admission signage work the same way: California law does not allow a waiver to excuse gross negligence, meaning a conscious disregard for a known safety hazard.

Corporate operators like Six Flags, Comcast/Universal, and Cedar Fair have claims-handling teams and defense counsel that respond quickly and aggressively to any reported injury. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after an amusement park or venue injury.

Large operators activate a claims team immediately. The inspection permits and maintenance logs that prove negligence can be purged on a routine schedule if you don't act fast.

1
At the scene
Report the injury to park or venue security and get a written incident report.

Every theme park and major venue is required to log guest injuries. Ask for the incident report number, the names of the security or guest services staff who responded, and the specific ride or location involved. Do not leave the property without this documentation — operators sometimes decline to provide it unless specifically asked.

2
At the scene
Photograph the ride, restraint system, signage, and your injuries.

Document the specific restraint or safety mechanism involved, any visible mechanical wear or damage, posted warning signage, and the general condition of the area. If the injury happened in a common area, photograph the walkway, lighting, or crowd conditions that contributed. These conditions are often corrected within hours.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved before records are purged.

Corporate operators like Six Flags, Universal, and Cedar Fair have claims teams that respond quickly to any reported injury. Their job is to control the narrative and limit exposure. Having your own attorney send a preservation letter immediately locks down the Cal/OSHA inspection permit, daily maintenance logs, prior incident reports, and surveillance footage before anything is altered or routinely deleted.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment and follow through.

Ride-related trauma and crowd-related injuries can involve internal injuries, spinal compression, or concussion that don't present fully until hours later. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this specific incident, not a pre-existing condition an insurer will try to blame.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement or sign anything from the operator's insurer.

The park or venue's insurer will often contact you quickly, sometimes offering a small settlement or asking you to sign a release in exchange for comped tickets or a refund. Signing anything before you know the full extent of your injuries can waive your claim. Let your attorney handle all communication with the operator and its insurer.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document lost income.

Ride and venue injuries can require extended treatment, from orthopedic surgery to physical therapy. Every medical visit, prescription, and day of missed work builds the damages case. Keep a file of everything and share it with your attorney regularly.

What ride & venue failures cause

A restraint or security
failure can be catastrophic.

Amusement rides operate at high speed and force by design. When a restraint, mechanical component, or crowd-control plan fails, the injuries reflect the forces involved.

Traumatic Brain Injury
Sudden jolts, ejections, or falls from a ride or bleacher can cause concussion and more severe traumatic brain injury. Head strikes during crowd surges at concerts and stadiums produce the same risk.
Spinal Cord Injuries
A failed restraint that allows a guest to shift or eject during a high-speed ride transmits extreme force through the spine. Vertebral fractures and spinal cord damage can result in partial or complete paralysis.
Crush & Amputation Injuries
Mechanical entanglement in ride components, gates, and moving platforms can cause severe crush injuries or traumatic amputation. These cases turn heavily on maintenance and pre-opening inspection records.
Fractures & Dislocations
Sudden stops, emergency ride evacuations, and falls in crowded common areas produce fractures and joint dislocations. Femur, wrist, and ankle injuries are especially common in evacuation and crowd-control incidents.
Whiplash & Neck Injuries
High-g-force rides with sudden acceleration or deceleration can cause whiplash and cervical spine injury, particularly when a restraint system fails to keep the head and neck properly stabilized throughout the ride cycle.
Lacerations & Traumatic Wounds
Structural failures, exposed mechanical components, and debris from a malfunctioning ride can cause severe lacerations. These injuries often require surgical repair and leave permanent scarring.
What they don't want you to know

The operator's claims team has a playbook. So do we.

Six Flags, Universal, Cedar Fair, and stadium operators carry large insurance policies and experienced defense teams. Here's what they typically argue in amusement park and venue cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"You signed a waiver — you can't sue"
Operators lean hard on the release language printed on tickets and admission signage. California law does not allow a waiver to excuse gross negligence — a conscious disregard for a known safety hazard, such as operating a ride with a reported malfunction or skipping a required inspection. A waiver may limit claims for the ride's inherent risks, but it does not immunize negligence.
Tactic 02
"This is an inherent risk of the ride you chose to go on"
Assumption of risk is a real doctrine, but operators stretch it to cover their own mistakes. It applies to the risks genuinely inherent to the activity — not to a defective restraint, deferred maintenance, or a ride run outside its Cal/OSHA permit conditions. Courts distinguish between the risk you accepted and the risk the operator negligently created.
Tactic 03
"This ride passed its Cal/OSHA inspection this year"
An annual state permit under Labor Code § 7920 is a floor, not proof the ride was safe on the day you were hurt. Daily pre-opening inspection logs and maintenance records — not the annual permit — show whether the ride was properly maintained between state visits. We subpoena both.
Tactic 04
"You didn't follow the posted safety instructions"
Operators point to warning signage and verbal instructions to shift blame onto the guest. Posted warnings do not excuse an operator from properly maintaining equipment or training staff — and under California's pure comparative fault rule, any fault attributed to you reduces your recovery, it does not eliminate it.
