California premises liability law

Property owners knew — or should have known.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of care on every property owner — commercial, residential, and government. When a dangerous condition exists and an owner fails to repair it or warn visitors, they are liable for the injuries that result. This duty covers not just slip-and-fall hazards but the full spectrum of unsafe property conditions: inadequate security that allows criminal attacks, defective pools, structural failures, dog bites, and toxic exposure.

The most common defenses — "open and obvious," "we didn't know," "the criminal is responsible" — all have significant limits under California law. Property owners are liable when they created the dangerous condition and when they had constructive notice, meaning they should have known through reasonable inspection. We investigate maintenance records, prior incident reports, crime history, and inspection logs to prove what the owner knew and when.

Premises liability cases produce some of the most severe and varied injuries we handle — from dog bites causing permanent facial scarring to pool accidents causing catastrophic hypoxic brain injuries. The stakes are high and defense opposition is aggressive. Evidence disappears fast. Surveillance footage is overwritten in 24–72 hours. We issue preservation letters the same day you hire us.

Law Dog handles all premises liability cases on pure contingency. We advance all costs and collect only from the recovery. If the dangerous property involves a government entity — a city park, public school, or county facility — Government Code § 911.2 gives you just six months to file. Contact us now.

Property owner playbook exposed

What every premises
liability insurer is trained to say.

Property owners and their insurers have a standard script for premises liability claims. It starts with the assumption that the danger wasn't their fault — and works backward. We've heard every argument and know exactly how to counter each one.

Tactic 01
"It was open and obvious — you should have avoided it."
The open and obvious doctrine has significant limits under California law. Courts consider whether the visitor was distracted, whether lighting made the hazard non-obvious, and whether the owner should have anticipated exposure. Even when a hazard is visible, owners still have a duty to eliminate it if it creates an unreasonable risk — the doctrine is a comparative fault argument, not a complete defense.
Tactic 02
"We had no notice of the dangerous condition."
Property owners are liable when they created the condition and when they had constructive notice — meaning they should have known through reasonable inspection. Prior complaints, maintenance logs, and code violations establish this. We investigate every record the owner wants to hide.
Tactic 03
"The criminal is responsible — not us." (Negligent security)
Insurers claim the criminal — not the owner — is responsible. California law holds property owners liable for foreseeable criminal acts when they failed to provide adequate security. Prior crime history in the area puts the owner on notice — and they are liable when they ignore it.
Tactic 04
"You were trespassing — we owe you nothing."
Even trespassers have legal protection in California. Owners cannot set traps or act with willful or wanton misconduct. For child trespassers, the attractive nuisance doctrine applies to dangerous conditions like pools and equipment. We evaluate your exact status and identify every applicable exception.
Tactic 05
"You contributed to your own injury."
Defendants inflate your share of fault to reduce the payout. California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2) means this only reduces your recovery — not eliminates it. We document the dangerous condition thoroughly to minimize any fault allocation to you.
Tactic 06
"The condition was temporary — just appeared moments ago."
Owners claim the hazard just appeared and they had no time to address it. Surveillance footage often disproves this. We act immediately to preserve video before 30-day retention cycles purge it — and we subpoena cleaning logs and inspection records to establish the timeline.

Frequently asked

Questions people ask us about premises liability claims.

Premises liability is the legal doctrine holding property owners and occupiers responsible for injuries caused by unsafe conditions on their property. Under Civil Code § 1714, owners must use ordinary care to maintain property in a reasonably safe condition. This covers residential homes, apartment buildings, commercial properties, parking garages, retail stores, restaurants, hotels, amusement parks, and government-owned land.
You must prove four elements: (1) the defendant owned, leased, or controlled the property; (2) the defendant was negligent in maintaining it — meaning they created a dangerous condition, knew about it, or should have known about it with reasonable inspection; (3) you suffered harm; and (4) the dangerous condition was a substantial factor in causing your harm.
Yes. Civil Code § 3342 imposes strict liability on dog owners when their dog bites someone in a public place or lawfully on private property. Unlike many states, California does not require proof that the owner knew the dog was dangerous — the first bite is enough to trigger full liability. Strict liability covers bites specifically; other dog-related injuries are typically analyzed under general negligence.
The general statute of limitations is two years from the date of injury under CCP § 335.1. If the dangerous property is owned or maintained by a government entity — a city park, public school, LADOT facility — you have only six months to file a government tort claim under Government Code § 911.2. Missing the government deadline is almost always fatal to your claim.
Even trespassers have legal protection in California. Owners cannot set traps or act with willful or wanton misconduct toward trespassers. For child trespassers, the attractive nuisance doctrine applies — if a dangerous condition like a pool or open structure is likely to attract children, the owner must take reasonable steps to protect them. We evaluate the circumstances and applicable exceptions to determine whether your status limits recovery.
Negligent security claims arise when a property owner fails to provide adequate security and a third party injures you on their property. Common scenarios include assaults in parking garages, robberies in apartment common areas, and attacks in hotel hallways. To establish liability, we look at whether prior criminal incidents in the area put the owner on notice, and whether proper lighting, cameras, locks, and security personnel were deployed.

Landlords and property management companies owe tenants and guests a duty to maintain common areas in reasonably safe condition — hallways, stairwells, parking lots, laundry rooms, pools, and elevators. If a dangerous condition existed that the landlord knew about or should have discovered through reasonable inspection, and that condition caused your injury, you have a viable premises liability claim.
