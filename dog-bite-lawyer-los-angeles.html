Dog bites & animal attacks

Strict liability. One statute. No excuses for the owner.

California Civil Code § 3342 makes dog owners strictly liable for bites — regardless of whether they knew the dog was dangerous, regardless of whether the dog had ever bitten before. This is a fundamental departure from the "one bite" rule that still governs most states. In California, the first bite is fully compensable. The owner cannot escape liability by claiming surprise at their dog's behavior. The statute requires only that the bite occurred in a public place or anywhere the victim had a right to be.

Liability can extend beyond the dog's direct owner. A landlord who knew a tenant's dog was dangerous and had the power to require its removal may share liability. A property owner who permitted an aggressive animal on their premises can be brought into the case. We investigate every party with potential exposure — because policy limits matter, and identifying all available coverage maximizes your recovery.

Dog bite victims are entitled to the full spectrum of damages: emergency and ongoing medical care, wound closure and reconstructive surgery, scarring and disfigurement, lost wages, PTSD and psychological trauma, and pain and suffering. Permanent facial scarring — disproportionately suffered by children — has substantial independent compensable value under California law, and juries respond to it.

Evidence disappears quickly in dog bite cases. Animal Control bite records can be purged. Witnesses move on. The dog's prior history of aggression and the owner's knowledge of prior incidents are hardest to establish as time passes. Start your case today — we pull bite records, review vaccination history, and identify prior incidents before they become unavailable.

Step-by-step

What to do after a dog bite.

The actions you take in the first 48 hours after a dog bite have an outsized effect on your recovery — both medical and legal. Follow these steps in order.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Get the owner's information.

Name, address, phone, and insurance. Ask if the dog is vaccinated and get the vet's name. If the owner fled, get witness contact information immediately. This information is the foundation of your claim — without it, locating an insurer becomes significantly harder.

2
At the scene
Photograph the injuries before cleaning.

Bite wounds, puncture marks, tearing, bruising — photograph everything before any wound care. These images are among the most powerful evidence in a dog bite case. The raw, undoctored wound photographs taken at the scene establish severity in a way that clinical photos taken later cannot replicate.

3
Within 24 hours
Seek emergency medical care.

Dog bites carry serious infection risk including Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga, and in rare cases rabies. Get a full wound evaluation, document every injury, and follow all treatment instructions. The medical records establish the severity of your injuries and are the backbone of your damages claim.

4
Within 48 hours
Report the bite to Animal Control.

Report the bite to Los Angeles County Animal Control (or your city's equivalent). Animal Control will investigate, pull the dog's bite history, and create an official report. This record of prior incidents is powerful evidence — and it becomes unavailable if you don't report. Prior bite history, while not required to establish liability, strengthens your case and affects damages.

5
Before you sign anything
Do not give a recorded statement to the owner's insurer.

The owner's homeowner insurer will call — friendly, sympathetic, concerned. They will ask questions designed to establish provocation: "was the dog playing with you?", "did you reach toward it?", "did you make sudden movements?" These recorded answers minimize your recovery. Call us before you say a word.

6
Document your recovery
Photograph the healing process every few days.

Scarring is a major damage category in dog bite cases and courts respond to a documented progression. Keep a pain journal noting daily symptoms, sleep disruption, anxiety, and any fear responses. These contemporaneous records — not written weeks later — are what expert witnesses and juries find credible.

Common injuries

What a dog bite
actually does.

Dog bite injuries range from infections to permanent facial scarring to PTSD. We know which specialists to retain and how to document what insurance adjusters dismiss.

Puncture Wounds & Lacerations
Dog teeth apply extreme localized pressure, creating deep puncture tracks that may not bleed heavily externally but carry high infection risk. Lacerations from tearing are common on extremities and the face. Surgical debridement and closure are frequently required.
Facial Injuries & Scarring
Children are disproportionately bitten on the face due to their height relative to dogs. Facial lacerations, avulsion injuries, and ear/nose/lip injuries can require multiple reconstructive surgeries. Permanent scarring to the face has substantial independent compensable value in California.
Nerve Damage
Deep puncture wounds can lacerate or compress peripheral nerves, causing numbness, weakness, and chronic pain in the affected limb. Nerve injuries from dog bites often require specialist evaluation and may not fully resolve.
Infection & Sepsis
Dog mouths carry polymicrobial bacteria. Infection rates for dog bite wounds are significant — particularly for immunocompromised individuals. Severe infections can progress to cellulitis, osteomyelitis (bone infection), or sepsis requiring hospitalization and IV antibiotics.
Psychological Trauma / PTSD
Dog bite victims frequently develop post-traumatic stress responses: fear of dogs, hypervigilance outdoors, nightmares, and panic attacks in situations involving animals. PTSD from animal attacks is well-documented and fully compensable under California law.
Rabies Exposure
Though rare, rabies exposure from a bite of unknown vaccination status requires post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) — a series of injections over 14 days. PEP costs $3,000–$10,000. The cost is recoverable, as is the fear and disruption of the treatment protocol.
What they don't want you to know

The playbook they run on dog bite victims.

Homeowner insurance adjusters follow a script designed to minimize dog bite payouts. Knowing what's coming is the first defense.

Tactic 01
The Provocation Defense
"You must have provoked the dog." This is the owner's primary shield under Civil Code § 3342. They'll argue you reached toward the dog, made sudden movements, startled it, or invaded its space. We document the actual circumstances — witness statements, surveillance, the dog's position and behavior — to counter false provocation narratives.
Tactic 02
Minimizing Scarring Value
Homeowner insurers routinely lowball scar claims by arguing scars will fade, the victim is too young to claim permanent disfigurement, or that the bite was "minor." California juries have historically valued permanent facial and body scarring significantly. We retain plastic surgery experts who document and project the long-term appearance and functional impact.
Tactic 03
The "No Prior History" Defense
"Our dog has never bitten anyone before." Under strict liability, this is legally irrelevant to the owner's liability — but insurers raise it to minimize settlement pressure. We don't need prior incidents to establish your case. We do investigate prior incidents because they affect punitive damages and support the narrative of a dangerous animal.
Tactic 04
Delayed Coverage Acknowledgment
Homeowner insurers often initially claim the policy may not cover the incident — off-premises bites, renters versus owners. Most standard California homeowner and renter policies DO cover dog bites. We identify all available coverage — homeowner, renter, umbrella — before making any demand.
