T-bone & intersection crashes

The deadliest dispute in personal injury law.

A T-bone crash — where one vehicle's front end strikes the side of another — is one of the most catastrophic collision types on California roads. The side door provides almost no engineered crumple zone between an impacting bumper and the occupant's body. Depending on the closing speed and angle, a T-bone can produce traumatic brain injury, internal organ damage, spinal cord injury, and hip or pelvic fractures — injuries that may not be immediately apparent in the emergency room.

What makes these cases particularly complex is that fault is almost always disputed. Both drivers claim they had the green light or the right of way. Both are usually telling the truth as they experienced it — because intersection perception at high speed is genuinely difficult. The truth of what happened is in the evidence: traffic camera footage, red light camera data, event data recorder readings, skid mark geometry, point of impact analysis, and witness accounts.

The problem: much of that evidence disappears within hours. Private surveillance cameras at adjacent businesses typically record on loops as short as 24 hours. LADOT traffic management cameras may overwrite similarly. Every hour without a preservation demand is an hour of evidence gone. From the moment you retain us, we send legal preservation letters to every relevant entity — city agencies, the California Highway Patrol, private businesses — demanding footage be held and not overwritten. This is the single most important step in a T-bone case, and we take it immediately.

If the signal was malfunctioning, or if the government vehicle caused the crash, additional claim requirements apply on an accelerated timeline. Contact us today — the clock on evidence starts at the moment of impact.

Step-by-step

What to do after a T-bone crash.

The first 24 hours after a T-bone are the most evidence-rich — and the most time-sensitive. Camera footage is still on the loop. Witnesses are still locatable. Skid marks are still on the pavement. Move fast.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Call 911. Don't move the vehicles.

A police report at the scene is essential. Officers document the position of the vehicles, note visible signal and pavement markings, and often include a preliminary fault determination. If the vehicles are moved before the officer arrives, critical position evidence disappears. Request that the officer note the signal status in the report — whether the light was functioning and what color it showed.

2
At the scene
Photograph everything, immediately.

Before vehicles are moved: photograph the exact resting positions of both vehicles, the point of impact on each, all visible skid marks or tire tracks, the traffic signals at the intersection from all four approaches, and any adjacent businesses or traffic cameras in view. The location and direction of the skid marks establish which driver braked and when — that evidence is destroyed the moment a tow truck moves the cars and street sweepers clear the pavement.

3
At the scene
Get every witness's name and number.

In a T-bone with disputed signals, witnesses are often the deciding factor. Get them before they leave the scene — many won't come forward later, and some will move or change phones within weeks. Ask responding officers to include witness information in their report. A single credible witness who says "I saw him run the red" is worth more than almost any other piece of evidence.

4
Within hours
Call us — we send preservation letters immediately.

The moment you retain us, we identify every possible camera source at and around the intersection — red light cameras (managed by the city), LADOT traffic management cameras, private business cameras, and residential cameras — and send legal preservation letters demanding the footage be held and not overwritten. We also request the event data recorder (black box) data from both vehicles before insurers can access and potentially dispute it. This step has a closing window measured in hours, not days.

5
Within 24 hours
Get emergency medical care — even if you feel okay.

Traumatic brain injury, internal organ injury, and spinal cord damage frequently present with a delay or are masked at the scene by adrenaline. Being evaluated in the ER creates a medical record that ties your injuries to the crash date. If the ER clears you but symptoms develop over the following days — headache, abdominal pain, weakness, confusion — go back immediately and tell your doctor about the crash.

6
Before you speak to anyone
Don't discuss fault — with anyone.

Not at the scene, not with the responding officer's open mic running, not on social media, and especially not in a recorded statement with the other driver's insurer. In a disputed-fault case, anything you say about the signal, your speed, or your view of the intersection can be used against you. Decline all recorded statements politely and contact us first. We handle all insurer communication the moment you retain us.

Side-impact injury profile

What a T-bone does
to the human body.

A side door has no bumper, no front-end crumple zone, no meaningful distance between the intruding vehicle and your body. The injuries are severe, often internal, and frequently delayed in presentation.

Traumatic Brain Injury
The head strikes the door, door post, or window with sudden lateral force. TBI ranges from concussion and post-concussion syndrome to diffuse axonal injury — disrupting cognitive function, memory, balance, and emotional regulation. Many TBIs are missed in the ER and diagnosed only after symptoms emerge in the following days.
Internal Organ Damage
The spleen, liver, and kidneys sit directly behind the side door panel on the struck side. Laceration or contusion of these organs is life-threatening and often initially asymptomatic — abdomen may feel mildly sore before becoming an acute emergency. Any left-side or right-side abdominal pain after a T-bone requires immediate evaluation.
Broken Ribs & Sternum
Door intrusion directly into the chest wall fractures ribs and the sternum. Multiple rib fractures compromise breathing and can puncture a lung (pneumothorax). These injuries are painful, slow to heal, and significantly limit daily function for months — and are fully compensable.
Hip & Pelvis Fractures
On the struck side, the door and lower body absorb the full lateral impact. Hip fractures and pelvic ring disruptions require surgery, extensive rehabilitation, and often result in permanent gait changes. Elderly victims face dramatically elevated mortality risks from hip fractures sustained in T-bone crashes.
Spinal Cord Injury
Lateral compression of the thoracic or lumbar spine can cause disc herniation, vertebral fracture, or in severe cases, direct cord injury — producing partial or complete paralysis. These are among the highest-value cases in California personal injury law, and we handle them with appropriate aggression.
Head & Facial Lacerations
Side windows shatter differently than windshields — into larger, sharper pieces. Lacerations from tempered glass can be deep, involve facial structures, and produce permanent scarring. Facial injuries carry both economic (surgical) and non-economic (disfigurement) damages.
What they don't want you to know

How insurers fight T-bone claims — and how we counter.

Side-impact crashes with disputed signals are among the most aggressively defended claims in California. Know what's coming.

Tactic 01
Blame the Victim
"You entered the intersection too fast / late / on a yellow." Even in cases with clear camera footage, the at-fault insurer immediately blames the other driver to create comparative fault. Under California's pure comparative fault rule, even finding you 30% at fault reduces their payout by 30%. We anticipate this and document why the other driver bears the greater share — or all — of the fault.
Tactic 02
Camera "Unavailability"
Without a timely preservation letter, the insurer can truthfully claim no footage was available — because the loop overwrote it while they waited. This is not accidental. The strategy is delay. Our response is speed: we send preservation letters within hours of retention, making the insurer's later claim that footage "wasn't available" legally and factually difficult to sustain in court.
Tactic 03
Internal Injury Delay
When internal injuries aren't diagnosed at the ER — which happens often — the insurer argues the injury isn't crash-related. "If it were serious, you would have been diagnosed immediately." We counter with medical expert testimony on delayed presentation, mechanism of injury analysis, and the ER's documented limitations in identifying internal trauma without advanced imaging.
Tactic 04
Multi-Party Confusion
T-bone crashes frequently involve multiple potentially liable parties: the at-fault driver, their insurer, a municipality (if the signal was malfunctioning), and sometimes a vehicle manufacturer (if airbags didn't deploy). Insurers exploit this complexity to point fingers at each other. We identify every potentially liable party and pursue all of them simultaneously — so no one hides behind the others.

T-bone accident
FAQ.

Fault in a T-bone accident turns on which driver had the right of way. California Vehicle Code § 21453 governs red lights; § 21800 governs uncontrolled intersections. In practice, both drivers claim they had the green. Fault is established through evidence: traffic camera footage, red light camera data, event data recorders (black box), point of impact on both vehicles, skid mark trajectory, witness statements, and where available, signal-cycle timing records from LADOT. The more evidence preserved immediately after the crash, the clearer the picture — which is why we act within hours of being retained.
Physical evidence tells the story even without cameras or witnesses. The point of impact on each vehicle, combined with their final resting positions, establishes the geometry of the crash — which vehicle was traveling in which direction and at what relative speed. Skid marks (or their absence) show braking behavior. The event data recorder inside each vehicle typically records speed, throttle, braking, and steering inputs for the 5 to 10 seconds before impact. A qualified accident reconstructionist can build a convincing fault narrative from this physical record alone. We retain these experts routinely in disputed T-bone cases.

T-bone crashes are uniquely dangerous because the side door provides almost no crumple zone between the impacting vehicle's front end and the occupant's body. Common injuries include traumatic brain injury (head striking window or door post), broken ribs and sternum from door intrusion, internal organ damage (spleen, liver, kidney), hip and pelvis fractures, spinal cord injury, and severe lacerations from shattered side glass. Side airbags help when they deploy, but they don't deploy in every T-bone scenario and provide far less protection than frontal airbags. We regularly work with trauma surgeons and neurologists to document the full scope of these injuries.
Disputed fault is the norm in T-bone cases, not the exception. Both drivers almost always claim they had the green. This is precisely why evidence preservation matters so much in the first 24 to 72 hours. Comparative fault in California is pure — meaning even if a jury found you 20% at fault, you still recover 80% of your damages. Our job is to build the evidentiary record that pushes the fault finding toward the other driver. We prepare every T-bone case as if it will go to trial, which generally produces significantly better settlement offers before it ever gets there.
Time limit

Two years — or six months if a government vehicle was involved.

California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years from the crash to file. But if a city bus, public works vehicle, or any government-owned vehicle was involved, you must file a Government Tort Claim under Government Code § 911.2 within six months. Miss that window and your claim against the government entity is permanently barred — even if your injuries are catastrophic. If you're unsure whether a government vehicle was involved, assume the shorter deadline and call today.
