Motorcycle accidents

Lane splitting is legal. Insurer bias is not.

California is the only state where lane splitting is expressly legal (Vehicle Code § 21658.1). Riders here know their rights — but insurance adjusters know how to exploit perception. "Motorcyclists are reckless." "You assumed the risk." "You were speeding." These are scripts, not facts, and we dismantle them with evidence.

Motorcycle crashes produce injuries far more severe than car accidents at the same speed. There is no crumple zone between you and the road. Road rash, traumatic brain injury, spinal damage, and fractures requiring multiple surgeries are common outcomes. The medical costs — and the pain and suffering that goes with them — are correspondingly higher. So are the damages we pursue.

Law Dog represents motorcycle accident victims on pure contingency. We advance every litigation cost — accident reconstruction, biomechanical analysis, medical record compilation — and collect nothing unless we win. Our interests and yours are identical.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), you can recover even if you were partly responsible — and your share is often inflated by insurers looking to minimize payouts. If a road defect caused or contributed to your crash, Government Code § 911.2 gives you just six months to file against the responsible agency. Contact us now — evidence from crash scenes disappears fast.

Immediately after

What to do after a motorcycle crash.

The first hour decides more than you think. Evidence is perishable — tire marks vanish, cameras overwrite, witnesses leave. If you can do nothing else, do these six things.

Tell us what happened →
1
At the scene — immediate
Don't remove your helmet

Let paramedics do it. Removing a helmet after a crash risks aggravating a spinal injury. Turn on your hazards if the bike is operable. Move yourself out of traffic only if staying is dangerous — but leave the motorcycle exactly where it came to rest for the police report.

2
At the scene — immediate
Call 911. Get a police report.

An official police report is the foundation of your claim. Get the responding officer's name, badge number, and report number before you leave. In a motorcycle crash, the report often records whether the other driver said they "didn't see" you — one of the most valuable admissions in these cases.

3
At the scene — immediate
Document everything before it disappears

Photograph the road surface, skid and tire marks, debris, the final positions of all vehicles, traffic signals, signage, and any road defects. Photograph your gear — your helmet, jacket, and gloves show impact direction and force. Get witness names and numbers. Note any dashcam-equipped vehicles nearby.

4
Same day — critical
See a doctor. Don't wait.

Adrenaline is a powerful painkiller — riders routinely feel fine and discover serious injuries hours later. A same-day medical visit establishes causation and creates the record your claim depends on. A gap in treatment — even 48 hours — becomes an argument that your injuries aren't serious or weren't caused by the crash.

5
Within 24 hours
Preserve your bike and gear — don't repair anything

Your motorcycle and gear are physical evidence. The impact pattern on your helmet can establish how your head moved on impact. The bike's damage tells an accident reconstructionist exactly what happened. Do not repair, clean, or dispose of anything until we have documented it. Notify your insurer of the crash but say nothing else.

6
As soon as possible
Call Law Dog before you talk to any insurer

The other driver's insurer will call quickly — they want a recorded statement before you have legal representation. Do not give one. Do not accept a check. Do not describe your injuries as minor. Intersection camera footage is typically overwritten in 24–72 hours. The sooner we send preservation letters, the more evidence we can lock down.

What we document and fight for

Motorcycle injuries are
rarely minor.

Insurers routinely minimize the severity of rider injuries. We retain medical and biomechanical experts who document every consequence — present and future.

Road Rash
From superficial abrasion to full skin avulsion requiring grafts. Serious road rash causes permanent scarring, nerve damage, and infection risk. Insurers lowball these routinely — we don't let them.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Even helmeted riders sustain TBI in high-force impacts. Cognitive changes, personality shifts, chronic headaches, and memory loss are common long-term consequences with lifetime care costs.
Spinal Cord Damage
Partial or complete paralysis. Even incomplete spinal injuries can result in permanent weakness, chronic pain, and loss of function. Life-care planning experts establish the full scope of future costs.
Fractures
Collarbone, wrist, ankle, femur, and pelvis fractures are common in motorcycle crashes. Multiple fractures requiring hardware, lengthy rehab, and repeat surgeries significantly increase case value.
Amputations
Partial or complete limb loss, particularly of feet and lower legs in slide-out crashes. Prosthetics, adaptive equipment, home modification, and lost earning capacity are all compensable.
Internal Organ Damage
Blunt abdominal trauma can rupture the spleen, liver, or bowel — injuries that aren't always apparent immediately. Same-day imaging is critical to catching internal bleeding early.
Degloving Injuries
High-speed skin avulsion where skin and tissue are separated from underlying structure. Requires intensive wound care, multiple surgeries, and prolonged recovery. Permanently disfiguring.
Joint Damage
Knee, shoulder, and hip injuries from impact and landing. Torn ligaments, labral tears, and cartilage damage often require surgery and result in long-term instability or arthritis.
Facial Injuries
Road contact to the face causes fractures, dental damage, and scarring. In open-face helmet crashes, facial trauma can be severe. Reconstructive surgery costs and disfigurement are compensable.
Insurer playbook exposed

What every motorcycle
adjuster is trained to say.

Insurance companies have a script for motorcycle claims. It starts with the assumption that the rider was reckless and works backward from there. We've heard every line — and we know exactly how to answer each one.

Tactic 01
"Motorcyclists assume the risk of riding."
Assumption of risk is a doctrine that applies to known dangers inherent to an activity — not to other drivers' negligence. Choosing to ride does not release other drivers from their duty of care. California courts have consistently rejected this argument in motorcycle accident cases. It is intimidation, not law.
Tactic 02
"You were lane splitting — that's inherently dangerous."
Lane splitting is legal in California under Vehicle Code § 21658.1. Characterizing lawful behavior as negligence is a misrepresentation of the law. When a driver changes lanes without signaling or checking mirrors and strikes a lane-splitting rider, the driver is at fault. We document speed differentials and lane positions with expert reconstruction.
Tactic 03
"We think you were speeding."
This claim is almost always made without supporting evidence — it is designed to inflate your share of fault and reduce the insurer's payout. Physical evidence tells the truth: skid marks, impact damage patterns, and accident reconstruction establish actual speed. Bare assertions without evidence do not reduce your recovery.
Tactic 04
"You weren't wearing proper gear — that's on you."
California requires helmets (CVC § 27803), but no law requires a full jacket, gloves, or boots. Gear choice is relevant only to the extent that the specific injury might have been mitigated — and only to those specific injuries. It does not reduce recovery for fractures, spinal damage, or any injury unrelated to gear. We limit this argument aggressively.
Tactic 05
"Give us a recorded statement — just standard procedure."
It is not standard procedure. It is an opportunity to create a record they can use against you. Adjusters are trained interviewers. A casual phrase like "I'm okay" or "I didn't see them either" can significantly reduce your claim. You are not required to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurer. Decline and call us.
Tactic 06
"Here's a quick settlement — sign before you know your full diagnosis."
An early offer is designed to close the file before the full extent of your injuries is known. Once you sign a release, the case is permanently closed — even if your TBI symptoms worsen, your surgery is more complex than expected, or you discover you cannot return to work. Never accept a first offer without independent medical and legal review.

Filing deadlines

Two years. Six months for road defects. The clock is running.

Two years from the crash date under CCP § 335.1 for claims against a private driver. Six months under Government Code § 911.2 for any claim involving a government-maintained road, pothole, missing signage, or dangerous construction zone — including city streets, county roads, and state highways. Miss either deadline and the claim is permanently extinguished, regardless of merit. Evidence also disappears fast — intersection cameras typically overwrite in 24–72 hours. The sooner we start, the stronger the case.
