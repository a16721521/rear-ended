Apartment & residential slip and fall

When a landlord's deferred maintenance causes a fall, the maintenance records tell the story.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of reasonable care on every property owner — including landlords and property management companies. Civil Code § 1941 goes further, requiring landlords to maintain residential premises in a habitable condition — a duty that courts have applied to structural safety throughout the entire property. Broken stair treads, missing or wobbly handrails, cracked exterior walkways, pooling water in common-area corridors, malfunctioning elevators, and dark parking garages are all conditions that trigger landlord liability when someone is injured. The legal question is not whether you fell — it is whether the landlord met their duty.

The most critical distinction in apartment slip and fall cases is actual versus constructive notice. Actual notice means the landlord was directly told about the hazard — through a written maintenance request, a complaint from another tenant, or an employee who observed the condition. Constructive notice means the condition existed long enough that the landlord should have discovered it through reasonable inspection, even without being told. Maintenance request records are the most powerful evidence we obtain in these cases. A stack of unanswered work orders for a broken stair railing or a recurring lobby water intrusion problem establishes that the landlord knew — and chose not to act. Civil Code § 1942 gives tenants rights related to habitability remedies, and the same deferred-maintenance patterns that generate those claims are often central to personal injury liability.

Apartment falls often involve multiple potentially responsible parties: the individual property owner, the property management company they hired, an HOA responsible for shared common areas in a condo complex, and in some cases individual unit owners who created or had notice of a hazard. Los Angeles rent-controlled properties and Section 8 housing frequently show patterns of systemically deferred maintenance — a property-wide record of neglect that goes far beyond a single isolated hazard. We investigate the full maintenance history, not just the specific condition that caused your fall.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. The deadline to file is two years under Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1, but if your fall occurred in government-subsidized housing, the deadline may be six months under Government Code § 911.2. Step-by-step

What to do after a fall in an apartment building.

Landlords and property managers move fast after a fall — to document the scene in their favor and repair the hazard before it can be photographed. The maintenance records and surveillance footage that prove negligence can disappear if you don't act immediately.

1
At the scene
Report the fall to building management and request an incident report.

Notify the property manager, building supervisor, or leasing office and ask them to create a written incident report. Get a copy before you leave — or request one in writing the same day. This creates an official record the property owner cannot later deny. Never say you are "fine" or "okay" when you don't yet know the full extent of your injuries.

2
At the scene
Photograph the hazard and locate every nearby surveillance camera.

Document the specific condition that caused your fall — the broken stair tread, missing handrail, pooled water, cracked walkway, or malfunctioning elevator — before building management repairs or removes it. Photograph the surrounding area and note the position of all visible cameras. Note the time and exact location. Landlords and property managers frequently repair hazards within hours of a fall.

3
Same day
Get emergency or urgent care medical evaluation — don't wait.

Hip fractures, spinal injuries, and head injuries from falls are not always immediately apparent — adrenaline masks pain in the hours after a fall. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record that links your injuries to this specific incident. A gap in treatment — even 48 hours — becomes an argument that your injuries were not caused by the fall.

4
Within hours
Contact an attorney before the landlord's insurer calls you.

The property owner's insurer will contact you quickly — often within 24 hours — framing it as a routine call. Their goal is to get a recorded statement before you understand your rights, and to reach a settlement before the full extent of your injuries is known. Having your attorney send a preservation letter immediately protects the surveillance footage, maintenance records, and inspection logs before anything is altered, repaired, or lost.

5
Within days
Gather any prior maintenance complaints you submitted about this hazard.

If you previously submitted maintenance requests — in writing, by email, through an app, or to building staff — about the same condition that caused your fall, preserve every record. Print copies, take screenshots, and share them with your attorney. These records are among the most powerful evidence in landlord liability cases because they establish exactly what the landlord knew and when they knew it.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document every cost and missed workday.

Apartment fall injuries — hip fractures, spinal injuries, TBIs — often require extended treatment across months or years. Every medical visit, prescription, therapy session, specialist referral, and day of missed work builds the damages case. Keep a running file of everything and share updates with your attorney regularly. Do not accept a settlement offer before the full extent of your injuries and recovery time is known.

What apartment falls cause

A fall on a hard lobby floor
or down a stairway is not minor.

Apartment and residential property falls frequently involve hard tile, concrete stairways, and multi-story drops. The injuries reflect the surfaces and the forces involved.

Hip Fractures
The most severe and common fall injury, particularly when falls occur on hard lobby and corridor flooring or down stairways. Often requires hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation. Risk of secondary complications — including blood clots, pneumonia, and permanent mobility loss — is well-documented.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Head contact with tile, concrete, or stone floors in apartment lobbies and stairwells causes concussion, skull fracture, or hemorrhage. Cognitive changes, memory loss, and personality shifts may not appear for days or weeks after the fall — by which point an unrepresented claimant may have already accepted a lowball settlement.
Spinal Cord Injuries
A backward fall down a stairway compresses the spine on impact with each step, causing herniated discs, vertebral fractures, or spinal cord damage with partial or permanent neurological effects. Spinal injuries from stairway falls can result in paralysis requiring lifelong adaptive equipment and in-home care.
Wrist and Collarbone Fractures
The natural instinct to brace a fall with outstretched hands leads to wrist fractures — often comminuted (shattered) fractures requiring surgery and hardware. Grabbing for a missing or broken handrail during a stairway fall can also cause collarbone and shoulder injuries when the sudden load transfer fails.
Knee Injuries
Impact or twisting on slippery lobby tile, wet laundry room floors, or uneven parking garage surfaces can tear the ACL, MCL, or meniscus. These injuries frequently require surgery and extended physical therapy, with variable long-term outcomes that affect mobility and the ability to continue employment.
Ankle Fractures
Stepping onto cracked exterior walkways, uneven pavement at building entrances, or broken curbing in parking areas commonly causes ankle fractures, sometimes with ligament involvement. Surgery, casting, and limited mobility during recovery significantly affect employment and daily function for months or longer.
What they don't want you to know

The property manager's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Landlords and their insurers have standard defenses for residential slip and fall claims. Here is what they typically argue — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"We had no prior notice of that condition"
Landlords are liable both when they created the hazard and when they had constructive notice — meaning they should have known through reasonable inspection and maintenance. Maintenance request records often tell a very different story — work orders for the same broken stair or recurring water intrusion that were delayed for weeks or months establish exactly what the landlord knew and chose not to fix.
Tactic 02
"The hazard was open and obvious — you should have seen it"
California's open and obvious doctrine has significant limits. Courts consider whether poor lighting in a stairwell or parking garage made the hazard non-obvious, whether a visitor was reasonably distracted, and whether the landlord should have anticipated people encountering the hazard regardless of visibility. A missing handrail that is "visible" to a sighted person is still a dangerous defect the landlord is obligated to repair.
Tactic 03
"The HOA is responsible, not us"
Landlords in condominium buildings sometimes attempt to deflect liability to the HOA when a fall occurs in a common area. But the landlord's duty under Civil Code § 1714 to their tenants and guests does not disappear simply because the HOA also has maintenance obligations. Both the landlord and the HOA may bear liability — and we name all responsible parties to ensure the full scope of accountability is on the table.
Tactic 04
"The lease limits our liability for common area injuries"
Lease provisions purporting to waive a landlord's liability for their own negligence are void under California law. Civil Code § 1953 prohibits landlords from using lease agreements to exempt themselves from liability for negligent conditions in common areas. A waiver clause in a lease does not bar your premises liability claim. Insurers count on tenants not knowing this.
