California pedestrian & bicycle law

Unprotected. Unshielded. Every collision is severe.

Pedestrians and cyclists have no crumple zones, no airbags, and no metal shell. When a vehicle strikes them, the injuries reflect that reality: fractures, traumatic brain injuries, spinal cord damage, severe lacerations, and fatalities at speeds that would barely dent a car door. California law imposes the highest duty of care on drivers in relation to pedestrians and cyclists — but collecting that duty as compensation requires building a strong case fast.

Pedestrian and bicycle accident cases disappear quickly. Surveillance footage from nearby businesses is overwritten in 24–72 hours. Black box data from the vehicle can be overwritten. Skid marks fade. Witnesses move on. The at-fault driver's insurer will begin its own investigation immediately — one designed to minimize your recovery. We begin ours the same day we're retained.

Hit-and-run incidents — which are tragically common in Los Angeles — don't mean your case is over. California's uninsured motorist (UM) coverage applies to hit-and-run accidents. We identify every layer of applicable insurance and pursue all available recovery even when the at-fault driver is never found. Don't assume no driver means no recovery.

If a government road defect — a broken crosswalk signal, missing bike lane markings, deteriorated pavement — contributed to your accident, a six-month government tort claim deadline applies under Government Code § 911.2. That clock starts running the day of your injury. Call us immediately.

After the collision — what matters

Six steps that protect your pedestrian or bicycle claim.

Evidence disappears within hours. Insurance companies start working against you the same day. These steps taken immediately after a collision can determine everything about what you recover.

Tell us what happened →
1
Immediate — at the scene
Call 911 and get a police report

A police report creates a contemporaneous official record of the collision — location, parties, preliminary fault assessment, and witness information. Without a police report, insurers dispute the basic facts. For hit-and-run, a police report is also required to make a UM insurance claim.

2
At the scene — evidence
Photograph everything before the scene changes

Vehicle damage, tire marks, road conditions, crosswalk markings, traffic signals, street signage, your injuries, and the positions of all vehicles. Photograph surrounding businesses that may have surveillance cameras pointing at the scene. This information determines which footage we must preserve immediately.

3
At the scene — identify
Get driver and witness information

Collect the driver's full name, license plate, insurance company and policy number, and contact information. Get names and phone numbers from any witnesses present. Witnesses who walk away are nearly impossible to find later. Do not discuss fault at the scene.

4
Same day — medical
Seek emergency care, even if you feel okay

Adrenaline masks pain. Head injuries, internal bleeding, spinal injuries, and soft tissue damage often present hours or days after the collision. A same-day ER visit creates the medical record linking your injuries to the accident. Gaps in medical care are used by insurers to argue injuries weren't serious or weren't caused by the collision.

5
Same day — surveillance
Note every security camera near the scene

Businesses overwrite surveillance footage in 24–72 hours. We send preservation letters to businesses near the scene the same day we're retained — demanding they preserve all footage from before, during, and after the collision. A video showing the driver running a red light or failing to yield is often worth more than all other evidence combined.

6
Before everything else
Call us before you speak to any insurer

The at-fault driver's insurer will call quickly with questions and sometimes an early settlement offer. Early offers in pedestrian and bicycle cases are almost always far below actual damages. A recorded statement made without an attorney can permanently limit your recovery. Refer all insurer contact to us — free consultation, no obligation.

What the driver's insurer will claim

Every pedestrian and bicycle
claim gets the same script.

The at-fault driver's insurance company will work quickly to minimize your claim. They have done this thousands of times. Here is the playbook they run — and how we counter it.

Tactic 01
"You were jaywalking — you weren't in a marked crosswalk."
California law protects pedestrians at unmarked crosswalks — the extensions of sidewalks at intersections — not just painted ones. Even mid-block crossings don't eliminate driver liability; drivers must still use reasonable care. Under comparative fault, your recovery is reduced, not eliminated, by any fault attributable to you.
Tactic 02
"You weren't in a marked crosswalk and you had no right of way."
Even where a pedestrian doesn't have absolute right-of-way, California drivers must exercise reasonable care to avoid striking pedestrians. Vehicle Code § 21954 requires pedestrians crossing outside a crosswalk to yield — but drivers must still use due care. This is a comparative fault question, not a complete bar.
Tactic 03
"The cyclist ran the stop sign / red light."
Insurers routinely allege cyclists were at fault for traffic violations. We obtain intersection camera footage, dashcam recordings, and witness statements to establish what actually happened. When a driver didn't see the cyclist at all — the most common scenario — the allegation that the cyclist was moving illegally often collapses under examination.
Tactic 04
"It was a low-speed impact — your injuries couldn't be this severe."
Even low-speed vehicle impacts can cause serious injury to an unprotected pedestrian or cyclist. A 15-mph impact that causes minimal vehicle damage can throw a cyclist to the ground with enough force to cause a TBI or spinal injury. We retain biomechanical engineers who document the force dynamics and link the mechanism of injury to the collision speed.
Tactic 05
"You should have worn a helmet." (Bicycle cases)
Helmet use is legally required only for cyclists under 18 in California. For adult cyclists, the absence of a helmet is not negligence per se and cannot be used to establish comparative fault for the accident itself. When raised as a damages-reduction argument, we work with biomechanical experts to address whether a helmet would have materially changed the specific injury outcome.
Tactic 06
"The driver was never found — you can't make a claim." (Hit-and-run)
California law requires uninsured motorist (UM) coverage that applies to hit-and-run incidents even when the driver is never identified. Your own auto insurance policy — even if you were not in a vehicle at the time — may provide UM coverage as a pedestrian or cyclist. We identify every applicable policy and pursue the full available coverage.

Frequently asked

Questions people ask us about pedestrian and bicycle accident claims.

California Vehicle Code § 21950 requires drivers to yield the right-of-way to pedestrians crossing in any marked or unmarked crosswalk at intersections. When a driver fails to yield and strikes a pedestrian in a crosswalk, they are presumptively negligent. The driver can attempt to argue comparative fault — that the pedestrian stepped out suddenly — but this is a difficult argument when the pedestrian was lawfully in the crosswalk.
Hit-and-run claims can still be compensated even when the at-fault driver is never identified. California requires uninsured motorist (UM) coverage that applies to hit-and-run incidents. Your own auto insurance policy — even if you weren't in a vehicle at the time — may provide UM coverage for pedestrian and bicycle hit-and-run claims. We identify all available coverage and aggressively pursue the full recovery.
Yes. Cyclists have the same rights and responsibilities as motor vehicle drivers on California roads. When a driver negligently strikes a cyclist — whether through distracted driving, failure to maintain a safe 3-foot passing distance (Veh. Code § 21760), dooring (Veh. Code § 22517), or running a stop sign — the driver is liable for all resulting injuries. Bicycle accident injuries tend to be severe because cyclists have no protective shell.
Dooring occurs when a parked driver or passenger opens a car door into the path of a cyclist without checking for oncoming bicycle traffic. California Vehicle Code § 22517 prohibits opening a vehicle door on the traffic side without first checking for approaching cyclists. When a door strikes a cyclist or forces them to swerve into traffic, the person who opened the door — and potentially the vehicle owner — are liable for all resulting injuries.
California Vehicle Code § 21760 requires drivers to maintain at least three feet of clearance when passing a cyclist on the road. When a driver passes with less than three feet of clearance and strikes or forces a cyclist off the road, the violation of this statute is evidence of negligence — making it significantly easier to establish fault. We obtain police reports, witness statements, and any available footage to document the distance violation.
The general statute of limitations is two years from the date of the collision under CCP § 335.1. If the at-fault party is a government entity — a city bus driver, a municipal vehicle, or if a defective road condition on a government-maintained street contributed — you have only six months to file a government tort claim under Government Code § 911.2. Missing the government deadline permanently extinguishes the claim regardless of injury severity.
