DUI and impaired truck driver crashes

When an impaired driver holds a CDL, both the driver and the carrier that put them on the road answer for it.

Commercial truck drivers are held to a stricter standard than private motorists. Under 49 CFR § 382.201, a blood alcohol concentration of just 0.04% — half the 0.08% limit for private drivers — is sufficient to trigger a per se violation. Federal regulations under 49 CFR § 392.5 also prohibit operating a commercial vehicle within four hours of consuming any amount of alcohol. The lower threshold reflects the greater skill required to control an 80,000-pound vehicle, the significantly longer stopping distances involved, and the catastrophic consequences when control is lost.

When a crash occurs meeting FMCSA severity thresholds — a fatality, an injury requiring treatment away from the scene, or a vehicle being towed — 49 CFR § 382.303 mandates post-accident drug and alcohol testing. The carrier, not the driver, is responsible for arranging that testing within two hours for alcohol and 32 hours for controlled substances. A carrier that misses those windows — or fails to document why it could not test — has committed an independent federal violation.

Carrier liability often extends further through negligent hiring and negligent entrustment. Federal regulations under 49 CFR Part 391 require carriers to conduct pre-employment motor vehicle record checks covering the prior three years before putting a driver behind the wheel. When those records revealed a prior DUI conviction and the carrier hired the driver anyway — or when the carrier failed to run the required check at all — the carrier bears direct liability independent of any vicarious responsibility for its driver's conduct.

These facts can support punitive damages under California Civil Code § 3294 when the carrier's conduct was oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributed to you — it is never eliminated. Step-by-step

What to do after a DUI truck crash.

DUI truck crashes involve carriers with legal teams that activate the moment a crash is reported — and federal testing deadlines that start running immediately. Acting fast protects the evidence that makes these cases.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Call 911 and tell the dispatcher the driver appears impaired.

Police officers responding to a commercial truck crash will conduct field sobriety testing if impairment is suspected. Your report that the driver seemed disoriented, smelled of alcohol, or was behaving erratically gets that documented from the start. The responding officer's report — including any field sobriety test results, observations of impairment, or refusals — becomes foundational evidence in your case.

2
At the scene
Document the driver's appearance, behavior, and anything visible in the cab.

Photograph and video the driver's condition if it is safe to do so — appearance, unsteady movement, speech. Note anything visible in or near the cab: open containers, food or drink packaging, the driver's demeanor. Witness statements from anyone who observed the driver before the crash — at a truck stop, rest area, or along the route — can corroborate impairment. Write down your own observations in detail as soon as you can.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney on the phone — federal testing deadlines are running now.

Post-accident alcohol testing under federal law must be arranged by the carrier within two hours of a qualifying crash. Drug testing must occur within 32 hours. Once those windows close, the carrier must document why testing was not completed. An attorney involved early can issue a preservation demand immediately and — if the carrier failed to test — build the record showing that failure for use at trial.

4
Same day
Seek emergency medical treatment immediately.

DUI truck crashes are high-force collisions. Spinal injuries, internal bleeding, and traumatic brain injuries often present with delayed symptoms — a few hours of initial adrenaline masking significant harm. A same-day emergency evaluation creates the medical record linking your injuries to this specific crash, which is essential for establishing causation and damages. Do not wait to see how you feel.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the carrier's insurer.

Commercial carriers maintain experienced adjusters and claims teams who know how to extract statements that can later be used to minimize your recovery. In DUI cases especially, they will probe whether you had any warning, could have avoided the crash, or contributed in any way. These questions have legal significance under comparative fault analysis. Let your attorney handle all communication with the carrier and its insurer from the beginning.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document the full impact of your injuries.

DUI truck crash injuries frequently require extended care — surgeries, rehabilitation, and specialist treatment over months or years. Every medical visit, prescription, and therapy session builds your damages case. If a parallel criminal case is proceeding against the driver, track those developments with your attorney — plea agreements, convictions, and admissions in the criminal case can strengthen your civil claim and are often admissible as evidence.

What DUI truck impacts cause

An 80,000-pound truck driven
by an impaired driver is fatal.

An impaired truck driver reacts slower, brakes later, and steers erratically — compounding the already catastrophic physics of a loaded commercial vehicle. The injuries reflect both forces.

Traumatic Brain Injury
Impaired truck drivers often fail to brake at all before impact — delivering a full-speed collision rather than a slowed one. The unmitigated force transmitted through the vehicle produces severe concussive injury, diffuse axonal damage, and hemorrhage, even in belted occupants with airbag deployment.
Spinal Cord Injuries
High-force impacts with minimal pre-collision braking transmit maximum deceleration forces through the spine. Vertebral fractures, disc herniation, and spinal cord compression can result in partial or complete paralysis, with lifelong care requirements and significant future medical expenses.
Internal Organ Damage
Blunt abdominal trauma from steering wheel contact, seatbelt loading, and vehicle intrusion during high-speed impacts can rupture the liver, spleen, or kidneys. These injuries are often not immediately apparent and require emergency imaging to diagnose before internal hemorrhaging becomes life-threatening.
Multiple Fractures
The force differential between a loaded semi and a passenger vehicle is 30:1. Without the speed reduction that a sober driver's braking provides, that force is fully transmitted at impact. Pelvis, femur, upper extremity, and rib fractures frequently occur together, requiring multiple surgeries and extended rehabilitation.
Burns
High-speed DUI crashes carry elevated fire risk — ruptured fuel tanks, friction-ignited fires from slide-out impacts, and cargo fires in specialized haul vehicles. Burn injuries require specialized treatment centers, multiple reconstructive procedures, and often result in permanent disfigurement that significantly increases non-economic damages.
Psychological Trauma
Survivors of DUI truck crashes often experience more severe psychological consequences than standard collision survivors — knowing the crash was entirely preventable and caused by someone operating a deadly vehicle illegally compounds PTSD, anxiety, and depression. These are fully compensable injuries that we document with retained psychiatric expert evaluation.
What they don't want you to know

The carrier's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Trucking companies carry large insurance policies and employ experienced defense teams. Here is what they argue in DUI truck cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The driver's BAC was barely over the federal limit — it barely affected their ability to drive"
The 0.04% commercial threshold is a per se legal violation. A driver at 0.04% has violated federal law regardless of observable impairment — and research consistently shows that alcohol at any level above 0.02% increases crash risk by degrading attention, reaction time, and vehicle control. A CDL holder who consumed any alcohol before driving has violated the spirit of the federal program even if they fall below 0.04%.
Tactic 02
"We didn't know about the driver's prior DUI history — we're not responsible for what we couldn't discover"
Federal law required the carrier to check. 49 CFR Part 391 mandates pre-employment motor vehicle record checks covering three years of driving history before a carrier places a driver behind the wheel. A prior DUI conviction would appear in that check. If the carrier ran the required check and hired the driver anyway — or failed to run the check at all — it owns that failure directly. "We didn't know" is not a defense when the law required them to find out.
Tactic 03
"The driver was an independent contractor — the carrier isn't liable for his personal conduct"
Independent contractor status does not absolve a carrier of federal drug and alcohol testing obligations. The FMCSA's drug and alcohol testing regulations apply to carriers who use owner-operators and independent contractors — 49 CFR Part 382 expressly covers motor carriers who use such drivers. And California courts apply strict standards to independent contractor claims in trucking, frequently finding carrier-driver relationships that constitute employment for liability purposes.
Tactic 04
"The post-accident test results are challenged — chain of custody issues make them inadmissible"
FMCSA regulations specify precise chain-of-custody procedures for post-accident testing under 49 CFR Part 40. A carrier that allowed chain-of-custody failures to occur — through delayed testing or improper collection — has committed its own regulatory violation. The failure to properly preserve testing evidence, when the carrier had the legal obligation to do so, can itself be argued as consciousness of guilt and supports an adverse inference instruction at trial.
