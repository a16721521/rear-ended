Swimming pool accidents

When a pool lacks required safety features, the property owner is liable.

Property owners in California owe a general duty of reasonable care to keep their premises safe, and that duty is heightened for swimming pools because the risk of catastrophic injury is so foreseeable. Under Civil Code § 1714, an owner who fails to secure a pool, maintain the deck surface, post depth markers, or supervise pool use appropriately can be held liable for a drowning, a near-drowning, a diving injury, or a fall on a wet or uneven pool deck.

When the victim is a child, California recognizes the "attractive nuisance" doctrine — property owners can be liable for injuries to child trespassers if a hazardous condition, like an unfenced pool, would foreseeably draw children who can't appreciate the danger. Courts have long treated swimming pools as the classic example. An owner who leaves a pool unsecured is not shielded from liability simply because the child wasn't invited onto the property.

California's Swimming Pool Safety Act, Health and Safety Code § 115922, requires any residential pool built or remodeled after January 1, 1998 to include at least one of several drowning-prevention features: isolation fencing, removable mesh fencing, an approved pool cover, exit alarms, self-latching doors, or a pool entry alarm. Apartment complexes, HOAs, hotels, and short-term rentals booked through platforms like Airbnb are held to additional lifeguard staffing, supervision, and posted-rule standards. Defective drain covers that cause entrapment injuries can also trigger liability under the federal Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after a swimming pool accident.

Pool accident claims involve property managers and insurers who move fast to control the narrative. Surveillance footage and maintenance records can disappear if you don't act quickly.

1
At the scene
Call 911 immediately.

Drowning and near-drowning injuries require emergency medical response — call 911 first, before anything else. If the pool is at an apartment, hotel, or HOA property, also notify management or on-site staff so the incident is logged and, if a lifeguard is on duty, so they can respond.

2
At the scene
Photograph the pool area in detail.

Document the fencing and gates (or lack of them), any pool alarms, depth markers, the deck surface, drain covers, and any posted rules or warning signage. These details often disappear quickly — a broken gate latch can be repaired within hours of an incident.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved before the property's insurer arrives.

Apartment complexes, HOAs, and hotels carry liability insurance and often move quickly to control the scene and the story after a pool accident. Having your own attorney send a preservation letter immediately locks down surveillance footage, maintenance logs, and lifeguard staffing records before anything is altered or lost.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment and follow through.

Even a near-drowning that looks resolved at the scene can cause delayed hypoxic brain injury that isn't apparent for hours. A same-day medical evaluation, and follow-up imaging if recommended, creates the causation record linking any injury to this specific incident.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the property owner's insurer.

The insurer will often call quickly, framing it as a routine follow-up. Their goal is to get statements on record that shift blame — suggesting a lack of adult supervision or that the victim ignored posted rules. Let your attorney handle all communication with the property owner and its insurer.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document lost income.

Pool accident injuries, especially hypoxic brain injury and spinal cord damage from diving, often require extended treatment and rehabilitation over months or years. Every medical visit, therapy session, and day of missed work builds the damages case. Keep a file of everything and share it with your attorney regularly.

What pool accidents cause

A missing safety feature
can be catastrophic.

Swimming pool accidents involve a uniquely unforgiving margin for error. The injuries reflect that — from oxygen deprivation to spinal trauma to lacerations from defective equipment.

Drowning & Wrongful Death
Drowning can occur silently and in seconds, especially for young children. When a property lacked required fencing, a functioning alarm, or adequate supervision, the owner's failure to meet basic safety standards can be the direct cause of a preventable death.
Hypoxic-Anoxic Brain Injury
A near-drowning that cuts off oxygen to the brain for even a few minutes can cause permanent cognitive impairment, memory loss, and motor function deficits. Symptoms are sometimes delayed, making immediate and follow-up medical evaluation critical.
Spinal Cord Injuries
Diving into water that is shallower than expected — often because depth markers were missing, faded, or inaccurate — can cause vertebral fracture and spinal cord damage resulting in partial or complete paralysis.
Traumatic Brain Injury
A fall on a slippery pool deck, a diving board malfunction, or a slip on wet concrete near the water can cause a head strike leading to concussion or more severe traumatic brain injury.
Drain Entrapment & Lacerations
Defective or missing drain covers can create powerful suction that traps limbs, hair, or clothing, causing severe lacerations, internal injuries, or entrapment drowning. The federal Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act sets drain cover safety standards that owners must follow.
Fractures & Soft Tissue Injuries
Slippery pool decks, uneven pavers, and poorly maintained walkways around the pool cause falls that result in wrist, ankle, hip, and facial fractures, along with sprains and soft tissue damage requiring extended physical therapy.
What they don't want you to know

The property insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Apartment complexes, HOAs, and hotels carry liability insurance and experienced defense teams. Here's what they typically argue in pool accident cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The victim was trespassing — we owed no duty of care"
Owners routinely raise trespassing to avoid liability entirely. Under the attractive nuisance doctrine, a property owner who fails to secure a pool that would foreseeably attract children can still be liable even when the child wasn't invited onto the property. An unfenced or improperly gated pool creates exactly the foreseeable risk the doctrine exists to address.
Tactic 02
"The pool met code when it was built"
Owners often point to the pool's original construction date to argue compliance isn't required. The Swimming Pool Safety Act applies to any pool built or remodeled after 1998, and requires the safety feature to actually be present and functioning — not just installed at some point in the past. A broken gate latch or a disconnected alarm is not compliance.
Tactic 03
"Supervision was the parent's or guest's responsibility, not ours"
Owners try to shift blame entirely onto the victim or their guardian. Apartment complexes, HOAs, and hotels that operate a shared pool owe an independent duty to maintain physical barriers, functioning safety equipment, and, where applicable, adequate lifeguard staffing — that duty exists regardless of who else was supervising.
Tactic 04
"There's no record of any prior incidents at this pool"
A clean incident history doesn't mean the hazard wasn't foreseeable. Maintenance and inspection records often show a pattern of deferred repairs, expired safety certifications, or ignored complaints that establish the owner knew or should have known about the danger — even without a documented prior injury.

Questions & answers

Swimming pool accident FAQ.

California's Swimming Pool Safety Act (Health and Safety Code § 115922) requires that any residential pool built or remodeled after January 1, 1998, include at least one of several drowning-prevention safety features: an enclosure that isolates the pool from the home, removable mesh fencing meeting ASTM standards, an approved pool cover, exit alarms on doors leading to the pool area, self-closing and self-latching doors, a pool alarm that detects unauthorized entry into the water, or another feature approved by the local building official. Apartment complexes, hotels, and HOA-managed pools serving the public are held to additional staffing and supervision standards on top of these physical barriers.
The attractive nuisance doctrine holds that property owners can be liable for injuries to child trespassers if a hazardous condition on the property, like an unfenced pool, would foreseeably attract children who cannot appreciate the danger. California courts have long treated swimming pools as a textbook example. Even if a child was not invited onto the property, an owner who fails to secure a pool with adequate fencing, a self-latching gate, or another required barrier can be held liable for a drowning or near-drowning, because the risk to curious children was foreseeable and preventable.
Liability depends on where the accident occurred. Residential pool owners owe a general duty of reasonable care under Civil Code § 1714, including compliance with the Swimming Pool Safety Act's barrier requirements. Apartment complexes and HOAs can be liable for negligent lifeguard staffing, missing depth markers, broken pool gates, or failure to enforce posted rules. Hotels and short-term rental hosts on platforms like Airbnb owe guests a heightened duty of care and can be liable for an unsupervised pool, missing safety equipment, or failing to disclose a known hazard. Pool equipment manufacturers can be liable if a defective drain cover caused an entrapment injury under the federal Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act.
Trespassing does not automatically bar recovery. Under the attractive nuisance doctrine, a property owner who fails to secure a pool that would foreseeably attract children can still be liable even though the child was not invited onto the property. For adult trespassers, liability is harder to establish but not impossible, particularly where the owner had actual knowledge that people regularly entered the pool area and failed to take reasonable steps to prevent access. Each case turns on the specific facts, including the effectiveness of any fencing or barriers that were in place.

Two years from the date of the incident under California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 for claims against a private property owner. If the pool was operated by a public entity, such as a city-run public pool or a public school pool, the deadline is six months under Government Code § 911.2 to file an administrative tort claim before a lawsuit can even be filed. Surveillance footage and maintenance logs can disappear quickly, so acting fast matters regardless of which filing deadline applies to your case.
Time limit

Two years to file. Pool evidence disappears faster.

California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years from the incident date to file a personal injury lawsuit against a private property owner. But the evidence that wins pool accident cases — surveillance footage, maintenance logs, and lifeguard staffing records — is often overwritten, lost, or discarded within days if a preservation letter isn't sent immediately. If the pool was operated by a public entity, the deadline is six months under Government Code § 911.2. The sooner we start, the more evidence we lock down.
