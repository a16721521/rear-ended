Inadequate lighting & premises liability

Poor lighting doesn't just cause falls — it invites the crime that follows.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of reasonable care on every property owner, operator, and property management company — and that duty extends well beyond trip hazards. A parking structure, apartment breezeway, or exterior walkway left dark for weeks isn't just an inconvenience. It removes the natural deterrent that lighting provides and creates an environment where assault, robbery, and other violent crime become more likely. When an owner ignores a known lighting defect in exactly the kind of location where crime tends to occur, the law treats that as a decision with consequences.

This is where premises liability overlaps with negligent security law. Property owners are not insurers against every crime that happens on their land, but they are liable when a criminal act was foreseeable and they failed to take reasonable precautions. Foreseeability is typically established through prior criminal incidents at or near the property, police call history for the address, prior tenant or customer complaints about dark or unmonitored areas, and any internal security assessments the owner received and ignored. Fixing a broken light is one of the cheapest security measures available — which makes it one of the hardest defects for a property owner to explain away once notice is established.

Building and electrical code minimum lighting standards — set by the Los Angeles Municipal Code and the California Building Code — are powerful evidence of the standard of care for parking structures, breezeways, stairwells, and exit routes. But code compliance alone is not a safe harbor: a property with a documented history of nighttime crime may owe a higher duty than the bare code minimum. We see this pattern most often in apartment complex breezeways and parking garages, commercial exterior walkways, motel and extended-stay corridors, and alleyways adjoining retail businesses — locations where foot traffic is low, sightlines are poor, and a burned-out fixture can go unaddressed for weeks.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. If the property is government-owned, Government Code § 911.2 gives you just six months to file a claim. Step-by-step

What to do after an attack or injury in a poorly lit area.

Property owners move quickly to fix the lighting and manage the narrative after an incident. The maintenance records, complaint history, and crime data that prove they knew can disappear just as fast.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Call 911 and insist on a full police report.

An official report documents exactly where the incident occurred, that the area was unlit, and the responding officer's observations. Get the report number and the responding officer's name and badge number. This report becomes a foundational piece of evidence establishing both the crime and the conditions that enabled it.

2
At the scene
Photograph the lighting conditions once it's safe to do so.

Document the broken, missing, or dim fixture, the distance between working lights, and the surrounding area — parked cars, landscaping, blind corners — that contributed to the lack of visibility. Property owners frequently repair lighting within hours of an incident, so photographs taken at or near the time of the attack are the most important evidence you can capture.

3
Within hours
Report the incident to property management and request the incident report.

Notify the property manager, building security, or on-site staff. Ask them directly how long the lighting had been out and whether there have been prior incidents in the same area — their answer, or their refusal to answer, matters. Get a copy of any incident report before you leave, and never say you're "fine" before you've been evaluated.

4
Same day
Get medical treatment, including a psychological evaluation if needed.

Physical injuries from an assault or robbery need immediate documentation, and so does psychological trauma. A same-day medical record creates the causation link between this specific incident and your injuries — physical and psychological. Delayed treatment gives an insurer room to argue your injuries weren't caused by what happened, or weren't serious.

5
Within days
Get an attorney to send a preservation letter immediately.

Maintenance logs and work orders showing when the lighting was reported broken, prior complaint records from other tenants or customers, and police call history for the address are the core evidence in these cases — and they disappear fast. Surveillance footage is often overwritten within days. A preservation letter puts the property owner on formal legal notice to retain everything.

6
Ongoing
Do not give a recorded statement, and keep every record.

The property's insurer will contact you quickly, often trying to get you on record minimizing the danger or admitting you knew the area was unsafe. Let your attorney handle that communication. Keep every medical bill, therapy record, and note of missed work — this documentation builds the damages case as treatment continues.

What inadequate lighting enables

Darkness doesn't cause the crime.
It removes the deterrent.

When a property fails to light the areas where an attacker would otherwise be seen, victims face violent crime with no witnesses and no warning. The injuries reflect both the physical attack and its lasting psychological toll.

Traumatic Brain Injury
Blunt force trauma during an assault in an unlit area frequently causes concussion, skull fracture, or more severe brain injury. Because the attack happens without warning, victims have no chance to brace or defend themselves before the first blow lands.
Stab & Gunshot Wounds
Violent crime enabled by poor visibility and low deterrence frequently escalates to weapons. Penetrating injuries require emergency surgery, extended hospitalization, and often leave permanent internal damage or disfigurement.
PTSD & Psychological Trauma
Surviving an attack in a dark, isolated area frequently produces lasting psychological injury — hypervigilance, anxiety, and fear of parking structures, breezeways, or similar spaces. These injuries are real, diagnosable, and fully compensable with proper documentation.
Fractures & Blunt Force Trauma
Being physically overpowered, struck, or thrown to the ground during a robbery or assault produces fractures to the face, ribs, arms, and hands — frequently the result of the victim's own defensive efforts in a struggle they never saw coming.
Sexual Assault Trauma
Dark, poorly monitored common areas — parking structures, stairwells, breezeways — are frequently the site of sexual assault claims tied directly to inadequate lighting and negligent security. These cases require sensitivity, discretion, and an attorney experienced in this specific area of premises liability.
Secondary Falls in Unlit Areas
Fleeing an attacker or simply navigating a chronically dark area can also cause trips and falls. If your injury resulted from a fall rather than a criminal attack, our inadequate lighting slip & fall page covers that claim in detail.
What they don't want you to know

The property owner's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Property owners carry liability insurance and experienced defense teams. Here's what they typically argue in inadequate lighting and negligent security cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The criminal act was unforeseeable — we can't be blamed for someone else's crime"
Insurers lean hard on the idea that a criminal, not the property, is responsible. Prior incidents, police call history, and prior complaints about the same dark area establish foreseeability — and once foreseeability is established, the owner's failure to fix a known, low-cost hazard becomes the focus of the case.
Tactic 02
"We had no notice this area was dangerous"
A burned-out fixture that goes unnoticed by property management for weeks is itself evidence of a broken maintenance system. We subpoena work orders, maintenance logs, and prior complaint records to establish exactly how long the defect existed — and whether tenants or customers had already flagged it.
Tactic 03
"The lighting met minimum code requirements"
Post-incident lighting measurements showing bare code compliance are common. Code minimums are a floor, not a safe harbor — a property with a documented crime history can owe a higher duty than the minimum foot-candle standard. We retain lighting and security experts to establish what that property specifically required.
Tactic 04
"You chose to be in an area you knew was unsafe"
This argument shifts blame to the victim for the owner's own failure to fix a known hazard. Under California's pure comparative fault rule, it can reduce — but never eliminate — your recovery. The property owner had superior knowledge of the risk and the ability to fix it cheaply — and chose not to.

California property owners are not automatically responsible for a third party's criminal act, but Civil Code § 1714's duty of reasonable care extends to conditions that make crime foreseeable. When an owner knew or should have known that a dark parking structure, breezeway, or walkway created an elevated risk of assault or robbery — and failed to take reasonable, low-cost precautions like repairing lighting — they can be held liable under a negligent security theory. The legal question isn't whether the owner committed the crime, but whether they created or ignored the conditions that made it foreseeable and failed to act.
Courts look at the totality of the circumstances. Prior criminal incidents at or near the property — assaults, robberies, or attacks documented in police call logs and crime statistics for that address or block — are the strongest evidence of foreseeability. Prior complaints from tenants, customers, or employees about unsafe lighting or a lack of security are also significant. So are prior similar incidents the owner was told about, security consultant assessments recommending lighting or camera upgrades that were ignored, and the general character of the surrounding area. A single unrelated incident is weaker evidence than a documented pattern, but even one prior assault in the same dark corridor can be enough to establish notice.
