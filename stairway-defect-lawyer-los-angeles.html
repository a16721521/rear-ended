Stairway defect slip & fall

When a building owner's failure to maintain stairways sends someone to the hospital.

Stairway falls are among the most serious premises liability incidents we handle. The physics are unforgiving: a missed step caused by an uneven riser, a loss of balance from a missing or broken handrail, or a slip from a worn or slippery tread surface sends a person tumbling down multiple steps before they can arrest the fall. The injuries that result — hip fractures, spinal compression, head trauma, and wrist fractures from instinctive bracing — reflect that force. California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of ordinary care on every property owner to maintain their property in a reasonably safe condition, and stairways are not exempt.

The California Building Code establishes minimum standards for stairway construction and maintenance: riser heights must be consistent, tread depths must meet minimum dimensions, and handrails must be continuous, graspable, and properly anchored along the full length of any stair flight. These aren't aspirational guidelines — they are legally mandated minimums that building owners are required to maintain. When a commercial landlord defers repairs, an apartment complex ignores maintenance requests, or a retail property continues to use a stairway with a documented defect, they create the exact conditions that lead to falls.

In residential properties, the duty runs deeper. Under Civil Code § 1941, landlords are required to maintain rental properties in a habitable condition — and that includes keeping common-area stairways safe. When a tenant or guest is injured on a stairway that the landlord knew about but failed to repair, the prior maintenance requests and building management logs become powerful evidence of constructive notice. Expert testimony from a building inspector and a biomechanist can establish both the code violation and the mechanism of the fall.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after a stairway fall.

Property owners and their insurers move fast after a stairway fall — sometimes repairing or replacing the defect within hours to eliminate the evidence. Here is what to do before they get the chance.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Photograph the specific defect before you leave.

Document the exact condition that caused your fall — a missing or broken handrail, an uneven or cracked riser, a loose or torn tread surface, a slippery surface with no warning sign, or inadequate lighting in the stairwell. Photograph from multiple angles and distances. Include something for scale if possible. Property owners have been known to make repairs within hours of a fall specifically to prevent documentation of the hazard that caused it.

2
At the scene
Report the fall to building management and get a written incident report.

Notify whoever is in charge — the property manager, store manager, building supervisor, or front desk staff — and ask them to create a written incident report. Get a copy before you leave the property. This creates an official record the building owner cannot later claim does not exist. Do not say you are "fine" or "okay." You don't know the full extent of your injuries yet, and a recorded statement that you're uninjured can be used against you later.

3
At the scene
Get the names and contact information of witnesses.

Anyone who saw you fall, who saw the defect before you fell, or who heard you report the condition to building staff is a potential witness. Get names and phone numbers before they leave. A neighbor who walked past the broken handrail for weeks and never saw it repaired — and who can testify to that — is valuable evidence of how long the condition existed and whether the owner had notice.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment, even if you think you're fine.

Stairway falls frequently cause injuries that are not immediately apparent. Spinal compression from the impact of landing, head trauma from contact with steps, and internal joint damage from bracing a fall can all present with delayed symptoms. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record connecting your injuries to this specific fall — and a gap in treatment, even 48 hours, becomes a defense argument that your injuries were not caused by the fall.

5
Same day
Preserve your footwear — do not clean or discard it.

Your shoes are evidence. Building owners and their insurers frequently argue that the victim's footwear was inappropriate for the conditions — open-toed shoes, heels, worn soles — to shift comparative fault. Preserve the shoes you were wearing exactly as they were at the time of the fall. Photograph them before you leave the scene if possible. Do not clean them. Their condition at the time of the fall is evidence.

6
As soon as possible
Call an attorney before giving a statement to the property owner's insurer.

The building owner's insurance carrier will contact you quickly — often framing it as a routine call to understand what happened. Their goal is to get you on record making statements that minimize the defect, suggest you weren't paying attention, or indicate that your injuries are minor. Let your attorney handle all communication with the property owner and their insurer. Surveillance footage showing the fall — if it exists — must be preserved through a legal hold letter within 24 to 72 hours before it is overwritten.

What stairway falls cause

Tumbling down stairs
is rarely a minor injury.

A stairway fall compresses the injury across multiple impacts — the initial slip, the fall through multiple steps, and the final landing. The injuries reflect that cascade of force.

Hip Fractures
The single most common serious injury from a stairway fall, particularly in older adults. A lateral fall onto the hip causes the femoral neck or intertrochanteric region to fracture under the combined impact force. Hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation are common outcomes, and the risk of complications — blood clots, infection, and secondary fractures — is clinically well-documented.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Tumbling down a stairway frequently involves head contact with the steps, walls, or landing surface — sometimes multiple times in a single fall. These secondary impacts cause concussion, skull fractures, subdural hematomas, and diffuse axonal injury. Cognitive changes, memory loss, headaches, and personality shifts can appear days or weeks after the fall, making early medical documentation critical for causation.
Spinal Injuries
Impact loading across the spine from falling on steps can cause vertebral compression fractures, herniated discs, and in severe cases, spinal cord injury with permanent neurological deficits. The lumbar spine is particularly vulnerable when a person lands in a seated position after a backward fall. Chronic pain and limited mobility following stairway spinal injuries are frequent long-term outcomes.
Wrist & Arm Fractures
The instinctive response to a stairway fall is to reach out for a handrail that isn't there — or to brace against the steps or wall. This reflex produces classic FOOSH (fall on outstretched hand) fractures of the distal radius, ulna, and the small bones of the wrist. These fractures frequently require surgical fixation with plates and screws, and permanent reduction in grip strength and fine motor function is common.
Knee Injuries
Catching a foot on a defective tread or landing awkwardly on the knee during a stairway fall produces high forces across the knee joint. ACL, MCL, and meniscus tears are common — frequently requiring surgical reconstruction and extended physical therapy. Ligamentous instability following partial recovery limits mobility and affects the ability to use stairs, climb, and perform activities that require knee stability.
Ankle Fractures
An unexpected step height caused by a non-standard riser, or a misstep on a worn or slippery tread, commonly produces ankle fractures as the foot rolls under the falling body's weight. Trimalleolar fractures — involving all three segments of the ankle — often require surgical repair with hardware, extended non-weight-bearing recovery, and can result in chronic instability and early arthritis in the joint.
What they don't want you to know

The building owner's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Property owners and their insurers have standard defenses for stairway fall claims. Here is what they typically argue — and how we dismantle each one.

Tactic 01
"You should have seen the defect — it was obvious"
The open-and-obvious doctrine is the most common defense in stairway fall cases — but it has real limits under California law. Courts consider whether the victim was distracted, whether lighting made the defect non-obvious, and whether the owner should have anticipated that people using the stairway would encounter the condition. A worn handrail bracket that looks intact until you put your weight on it is not obvious. An uneven riser in a poorly lit stairwell is not obvious. And even when a defect is visible, owners still have a duty to fix it if it creates an unreasonable risk.
Tactic 02
"The stairway was properly maintained — we have inspection records"
Inspection records that show checkboxes completed without photographs or specific measurements are not evidence of an actual inspection. We demand production of the full maintenance history — not just the inspection log, but work orders, repair invoices, and prior tenant or visitor complaints. A pattern of checked boxes and no corresponding repairs is evidence that the inspections were cursory or falsified. Prior tenant complaints that were not addressed establish constructive notice regardless of what the log says.
Tactic 03
"You weren't using the handrail — that's contributory negligence"
Under California's pure comparative fault rule, even if you were not using the handrail, your recovery is reduced only by your percentage of fault — not eliminated. But more importantly: if the handrail was missing, broken, or not graspable, not using it is not negligence — it is the expected result of a non-functional safety feature. Owners cannot argue victim negligence for failing to use a handrail that the owner's own maintenance failures made unusable.
Tactic 04
"The defect was minor — it didn't meet the threshold for a hazard"
Property owners frequently minimize stairway defects after a fall — claiming the handrail was "slightly loose" or the riser variation was "within tolerance." A building inspector expert can compare the stairway's actual condition against California Building Code standards and demonstrate the specific deviation that made it dangerous. A biomechanist can explain exactly how that deviation caused the fall mechanics. "Minor" defects in stairways cause catastrophic injuries — and the code exists precisely to prevent them.

When a defect exists and the owner knew or should have known about it through reasonable inspection, and failed to repair it, they are liable for injuries that result. The California Building Code establishes minimum standards for riser height, tread depth, and handrail continuity — deviations from these standards are strong evidence of negligence.
Landlords owe tenants and their guests a duty to maintain common areas in a safe and habitable condition under California Civil Code § 1941. Stairways are a common area in nearly every multi-unit building — and landlords who fail to repair broken handrails, fix uneven risers, replace worn treads, or provide adequate lighting in stairwells face direct liability for falls that result. Unlike commercial property owners, residential landlords also face liability for conditions that tenants complained about and that were not repaired within a reasonable time. Maintenance request records, building management logs, and prior tenant complaints are key evidence in these cases.
