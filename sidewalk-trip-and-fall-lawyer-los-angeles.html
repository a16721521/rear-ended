Sidewalk trip & fall

The City had notice. The crack was there for months. That's the case.

Government entities that own and maintain public sidewalks — the City of Los Angeles, Caltrans, LA County — are liable for dangerous conditions under Government Code § 835 when they knew or should have known about the hazard and failed to repair it. The most common causes of sidewalk trip and fall injuries in Los Angeles are tree root upheaval that buckles concrete slabs, wide longitudinal cracks that catch shoe tips and cane tips, sunken or raised sections at sidewalk joints, and missing or noncompliant curb cuts that funnel pedestrians onto uneven surfaces. These conditions typically develop gradually — which means the City often had months or years of constructive notice before anyone was hurt.

Liability isn't always with the City alone. Private property owners abutting public sidewalks have maintenance obligations under Los Angeles Municipal Code § 62.104 — and when a tree in a private owner's parkway heaves the adjacent sidewalk slab, both the private owner and the City may share responsibility. Identifying the correct responsible party requires pulling ownership records, maintenance logs, and prior service requests — 311 complaints, inspection work orders, and records of any prior repair work at the location — before anyone has a chance to obscure the timeline.

Private property owners on whose premises sidewalk-adjacent hazards occur are governed by Civil Code § 1714, which imposes a duty of ordinary care. These cases carry a two-year filing deadline under CCP § 335.1. But when a government entity is involved, Government Code § 911.2 cuts that window to six months — and missing it permanently bars the claim regardless of how serious the injuries are.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced by any fault attributable to you — but never eliminated. Government entities routinely argue you were distracted or the hazard was visible. We counter with evidence. Contact us today — the notice timeline and 311 records must be preserved before the City has any reason to clean them up.

Step-by-step

What to do after a sidewalk fall.

Government agencies and their claims departments move fast. The 311 records, maintenance logs, and street-level imagery that prove your case can disappear — and the six-month filing deadline has already started running.

1
At the scene
Photograph the exact hazard before it is repaired.

Take photos of the specific condition that caused you to trip — the crack width, the height differential between slabs, the exposed tree root, the missing curb cut. Photograph from multiple angles, including a wide shot showing the surrounding sidewalk and street address or cross street. Note the exact location, time, and any landmarks that will allow you to return to the same spot. Cities sometimes repair reported hazards within days of a fall to reduce their exposure.

2
At the scene
Get witness contact information before witnesses leave.

Anyone who saw you fall, saw you trip on the hazard, or heard you describe the condition at the scene is a potential witness. Get full names and phone numbers before they move on. Witnesses who are not identified at the scene are extremely difficult to locate later, and their testimony — particularly about how long the hazard had been visible — can be decisive in establishing the City's constructive notice.

3
Same day
Report the hazard to the City via 311 — and keep the service request number.

File a 311 service request documenting the sidewalk hazard at the location where you fell. This creates an official record with a timestamp. More importantly, your attorney will use 311 to pull the entire history of service requests at that address — prior complaints from other pedestrians are among the strongest forms of constructive notice that the City knew about the condition before you were hurt.

4
Same day — critical
Get emergency medical treatment and document your injuries.

Sidewalk falls frequently cause hip fractures, wrist fractures, traumatic brain injuries from head contact with pavement, and ankle fractures. Even if you feel you can walk away from the scene, adrenaline masks pain, and fractures are not always immediately apparent. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record that links your injuries to this specific fall — and a gap in treatment, even 24 hours, becomes an argument that your injuries weren't as serious as claimed.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the City's claims department.

The City of Los Angeles's claims department will contact you. Their goal is to get a statement on record before you have legal representation — statements that can be used to argue you were distracted, the hazard was visible, or you were wearing inappropriate footwear. Government claims adjusters are experienced at eliciting information that helps the City deflect liability. Let your attorney handle all communication with the City and its claims examiners.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document every day you miss work.

Sidewalk fall injuries — especially hip fractures and spinal injuries — often require extended treatment, follow-up surgeries, and months of physical therapy. Every medical visit, prescription, specialist consult, and day of lost income builds the damages case. Keep a running record of how your injuries affect your daily activities, your ability to work, and your quality of life. Share it with your attorney regularly so the case value reflects every real consequence.

What sidewalk falls cause

Pavement is unforgiving.
So are the injuries.

A trip on uneven concrete sends your body forward with full momentum and no warning. The impact — hands, wrists, knees, hip, or head — is abrupt and hard. The injuries reflect that force.

Hip Fractures
The most catastrophic sidewalk fall injury, especially for older adults. A forward trip with a lateral landing compresses the hip with body weight and momentum. Displaced fractures frequently require total hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation — with significant risk of complications and loss of independent mobility.
Wrist & Hand Fractures
The instinct to break a forward fall with outstretched hands transmits the full force of the impact through the wrists. Colles' fractures — a backward-angled distal radius break — are the classic trip-and-fall wrist injury. Comminuted fractures require surgical fixation, and permanent reduction in grip strength and fine motor function is common.
Traumatic Brain Injury
When outstretched hands fail to fully absorb the impact — or when the fall is a direct head-forward pitch — the skull makes contact with concrete. Concussion, skull fracture, subdural hematoma, and diffuse axonal injury can result. Cognitive changes, memory loss, headaches, and personality shifts may not appear for days or weeks, making same-day imaging essential.
Ankle Fractures
A foot catching on a raised slab or crack often twists the ankle violently before the fall. Bimalleolar and trimalleolar fractures — involving both or all three bony prominences of the ankle — require surgical repair with plates and screws. Recovery requires non-weight-bearing status for weeks, followed by extended physical therapy.
Knee Injuries
Landing directly on the knee during a forward fall causes patellar fractures, meniscus tears, and ligament damage. A twisting fall can tear the ACL or MCL. These injuries often require surgery and months of rehabilitation, with long-term effects on mobility and the ability to stand, walk, and climb stairs.
Spinal & Neck Injuries
A hard landing — whether on hands, side, or back — can compress the spine, herniate discs, or fracture vertebrae. Cervical fractures from neck contact with pavement carry the risk of permanent neurological damage. Lumbar compression fractures cause chronic pain that can be permanent and significantly affects daily function, employment, and quality of life.
What they don't want you to know

The City's claims department has a playbook. So do we.

Government entities and their insurers defend sidewalk cases with a standard set of arguments designed to deny or minimize your recovery. Here's what they typically argue — and how we dismantle each one.

Tactic 01
"We had no prior notice of the sidewalk condition"
Government entities can only be liable under Government Code § 835 if they had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition. The City's first defense is always that no one ever reported it. 311 records, inspection logs, and adjacent work orders tell a different story — prior service requests, nearby repairs, and maintenance records showing the City knew the pavement was failing in that area. We pull the full history before the City has a reason to manage it.
Tactic 02
"The hazard was open and obvious — you should have seen it"
The open and obvious doctrine has real limits under California law. Courts consider whether the hazard was oriented in the direction of travel, whether it was visible from a pedestrian's eye level and gait, and whether a reasonable person would have noticed it without specifically looking down. A tree root crack running parallel to the walking path is far less visible than one running perpendicular — and the City cannot claim a condition was obvious when its own maintenance records show it was reported as dangerous.
Tactic 03
"You were looking at your phone or not watching where you were going"
This is a comparative fault argument, not a complete defense. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), even if you were partially inattentive, the City's liability for maintaining a hazardous public sidewalk is not eliminated. Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault — but the City cannot use your momentary distraction to escape responsibility for years of deferred maintenance. We establish the condition's severity and the City's notice period to anchor the primary liability where it belongs.
Tactic 04
"That sidewalk section is the adjacent property owner's responsibility"
Cities sometimes argue that the abutting private property owner — not the City — was responsible for the sidewalk segment under Los Angeles Municipal Code § 62.104. This can be true, but it doesn't end your case: it means you have a claim against both the City and the private owner. When a parkway tree owned by a private party caused root upheaval of city-owned pavement, both may share liability. We identify every responsible party and pursue each one simultaneously.

Questions & answers

Sidewalk trip & fall FAQ.

Sidewalk cases uniquely involve the question of which entity — the City of Los Angeles, Caltrans, the county, or a private property owner — is responsible for the pavement where you fell. When a government entity is responsible, Government Code § 835 imposes liability for a dangerous condition of public property, but Government Code § 911.2 requires you to file an administrative tort claim within six months of the fall — not two years. Missing that six-month window permanently bars the claim. Additionally, private property owners may bear liability for sidewalk segments abutting their property under Los Angeles Municipal Code obligations. Determining who owns and maintains the specific section where you fell is the critical first step.
Liability depends on which entity owns and maintains the sidewalk segment where you fell. The City of Los Angeles maintains most public sidewalks within city limits and is liable for dangerous conditions under Government Code § 835 when it had actual or constructive notice — such as prior 311 complaints, maintenance logs, or service requests referencing the location. Caltrans is responsible for state highway corridors. Private property owners abutting public sidewalks may bear liability under Los Angeles Municipal Code § 62.104, which requires them to maintain adjacent sidewalk in a safe condition. In many cases, multiple parties share liability — for example, when a tree in a private owner's parkway causes sidewalk upheaval on city-maintained pavement.
Six months from the date of the fall under Government Code § 911.2. This administrative tort claim must be filed with the City before you can file a lawsuit. The City of Los Angeles processes government tort claims through the City Clerk's Office. Missing this six-month deadline permanently bars the claim — there is no exception for ongoing treatment, delayed diagnosis, or a good faith belief that the deadline was longer. If you are unsure whether your fall occurred on city-maintained property, contact an attorney immediately — the deadline applies even before the issue of ownership is resolved.
Government entities are liable under Government Code § 835 only if they knew or should have known about the dangerous condition in time to have corrected it. Actual notice includes prior 311 service requests, complaints to the City, or work orders referencing the specific location. Constructive notice — what the City "should have known" — is established by showing the condition was visible and had existed long enough that reasonable inspection would have discovered it. Tree root upheaval, wide cracks, and sunken sections typically develop gradually over months or years, and may be visible in historical Google Street View imagery or satellite photos. We pull 311 records, City inspection logs, and adjacent work orders to establish the full notice timeline.

Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault but not eliminated. Government entities routinely argue that you were looking at your phone, wearing inappropriate shoes, or should have noticed the uneven pavement — a variation of the "open and obvious" defense. These arguments affect the allocation of fault, not your right to recover. We counter them with evidence showing the hazard was not reasonably avoidable, including how the crack or upheaval was oriented relative to your walking direction and how long the condition had been visible on public record.
The deadline depends on who owns the sidewalk. Six months from the fall under Government Code § 911.2 for any claim against a government entity — the City of Los Angeles, Caltrans, LA County, or any government agency. Two years from the fall under California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 for claims against private property owners. You should never rely on the longer deadline — the question of who owns the specific sidewalk segment is not always immediately clear. If you wait two years and it turns out the government was responsible, the six-month window is long closed. Call an attorney as soon as possible after any sidewalk fall.
Time limits

Six months for the City. Two years for private owners. The clock started the moment you fell.

Government Code § 911.2 gives you six months from the date of your fall to file an administrative tort claim against the City of Los Angeles, Caltrans, or any other government entity — a strict prerequisite to filing a lawsuit. Miss it and the claim is permanently extinguished. California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years for claims against private property owners. When it's unclear who owns the sidewalk section where you fell — and it often is — you need an attorney identifying the responsible parties immediately, not after the shorter deadline has passed.
