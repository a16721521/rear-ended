Parking lot premises liability

The lot condition, the maintenance record, and who knew — that's the case.

A parking lot looks simple. It isn't. Commercial lots are complex surfaces maintained by multiple parties — property owners, parking management companies, janitorial contractors, and sometimes government agencies — each with their own duty of care under California Civil Code § 1714. That statute imposes a duty of ordinary care on every person in the management of their property. When a pothole forms, asphalt heaves, standing water pools after rain, or a light fails in a parking structure, the responsible party is required to repair it — or warn visitors about it.

The most common defense is that the hazard was "open and obvious." But a pothole that is difficult to see in low light, a patch of standing water that blends with wet pavement, or a cracked approach to an ADA curb ramp is not automatically visible to a person navigating a busy parking lot at night. Under 28 CFR Part 36, commercial facilities must maintain accessible routes — including parking surfaces — in operable condition. Violations of this federal standard are powerful evidence that the property owner knew the surface was defective and failed to act.

Parking lot falls often involve a shared-liability structure that property owners exploit to escape accountability. The owner points to the parking operator. The operator points to the management company. The management company points back to the owner. Our job is to pull the maintenance contracts, lease agreements, and inspection logs that show which party was contractually responsible — and whether any of them actually performed their duty.

Los Angeles's seasonal rain events compound the problem. A parking lot that drains properly in dry conditions can develop dangerous pooling and slick surfaces after the first significant rain of the season — conditions that property owners know recur every year and are required to address. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced by any fault attributed to you — but never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after a parking lot fall.

Property owners and their insurers move fast. Surveillance footage is overwritten in hours. The hazard gets repaired before anyone can document it. Acting immediately — at the scene and in the days after — protects your claim.

1
At the scene
Photograph the hazard before anything changes.

The specific condition that caused your fall — the pothole, the pooled water, the cracked asphalt, the dark section of the lot — needs to be documented immediately. Take photos from multiple angles showing the hazard's depth, size, and proximity to where pedestrians reasonably walk. If the area is poorly lit, photograph that too. Property owners have been known to patch potholes the morning after a reported fall.

2
At the scene
Report the fall and get the name of who you told.

Report your fall to the property manager, parking attendant, store manager, or whoever has authority on site. Ask them to create a written incident report and get a copy before you leave. Note the name and title of the person you spoke to. This creates an official record the property owner cannot later deny — and establishes that they had notice of both the hazard and the fall.

3
At the scene
Identify surveillance cameras and note their angles.

Parking lots often have multiple surveillance cameras covering the driving lanes, pedestrian areas, and lot entrances. Note which cameras might have captured the hazard location and your fall. This information is critical for a preservation letter — because without one, the footage is typically overwritten in 24 to 72 hours. Do not wait to get an attorney involved.

4
Same day
Get medical attention immediately, even if you feel okay.

Parking lot falls on hard asphalt produce significant impact forces. Hip fractures, spinal compression injuries, and traumatic brain injuries from head contact with pavement are not always immediately apparent — adrenaline masks pain. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record that links your injuries to this specific fall. A gap in treatment becomes an argument that the fall wasn't as serious as you claim.

5
Within hours
Call an attorney before the property owner's insurer calls you.

The property owner's insurer will contact you quickly — often framing it as a routine "wellness check." Their goal is a recorded statement before you understand what your case is worth. Once you have legal representation, all communication goes through your attorney. We send preservation letters for surveillance footage and maintenance records the same day you hire us — before either can disappear.

6
Ongoing
Document everything and preserve what you were wearing.

Keep every medical record, prescription, physical therapy note, and specialist report. Document every day of missed work. Preserve the shoes you were wearing — insurers frequently argue that inappropriate footwear contributed to the fall, and your shoes are physical evidence. Photograph them while you're still at the scene if possible, and do not clean or discard them.

What parking lot falls cause

Hard asphalt at full body
weight is catastrophic.

A fall in a parking lot is not a minor event. The impact of a full-weight fall onto asphalt or concrete — often with no time to brace — produces the same injury patterns seen in high-energy accidents.

Hip Fractures
The most severe and common injury in parking lot falls, particularly among older adults. A sideways fall onto asphalt transmits force directly to the hip. Displaced fractures frequently require total hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation — with significant risk of secondary complications.
Traumatic Brain Injury
A backward fall onto asphalt or concrete — triggered by a pothole underfoot or a foot catching on cracked pavement — often results in head impact with no time to brace. Concussion, skull fracture, subdural hematoma, and diffuse axonal injury can result, with cognitive and neurological symptoms that may not appear for days or weeks.
Wrist and Arm Fractures
The natural reflex to break a fall with outstretched hands transmits impact force through the wrist, forearm, and elbow. Colles' fractures, Smith's fractures, and distal radius fractures from parking lot falls frequently require surgical fixation with plates and screws, with lasting effects on grip strength and fine motor function.
Knee Injuries
Stepping into a pothole or onto an uneven surface creates a sudden twisting or buckling force on the knee joint. ACL, MCL, and meniscus tears result — injuries that frequently require surgical reconstruction and months of physical therapy, with variable long-term outcomes that affect mobility and return to work.
Spinal Injuries
A backward or sideways fall on asphalt compresses the spine on impact, causing herniated discs, vertebral fractures, and spinal cord injury. Chronic back and neck pain following a parking lot fall is common, and spinal cord damage can produce partial or permanent neurological effects affecting sensation, motor function, and bladder control.
Ankle Fractures
Tripping on cracked asphalt, a wheel-stop, or an unmarked speed bump causes the ankle to roll or invert suddenly, producing fractures — sometimes with associated ligament tears. Bimalleolar and trimalleolar fractures require surgery and non-weight bearing recovery periods of eight weeks or more, affecting employment and daily function significantly.
What they don't want you to know

The property owner's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Commercial property owners carry large liability policies and experienced defense teams. Here is what they typically argue in parking lot fall cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The hazard was open and obvious — you should have seen it."
This is the most common argument in parking lot cases. But "open and obvious" has real limits under California law. A pothole at night in a poorly lit lot, standing water that blends with wet pavement, or a cracked asphalt approach to a ramp is not obvious to a person moving through a busy commercial area. Courts consider the lighting conditions, the distraction inherent in a parking environment, and whether a reasonable person would have noticed the specific hazard in those specific conditions.
Tactic 02
"You weren't watching where you were going."
Insurers argue that a pedestrian in a parking lot has an obligation to watch for surface hazards. But parking lots are designed for distraction — people carry bags, unload children, use their phones, return shopping carts. Property owners know their lots are used by distracted pedestrians and have a duty to maintain surfaces safe enough for normal human behavior. Comparative fault may reduce your recovery, but it does not eliminate it under Civil Code § 1431.2.
Tactic 03
"We didn't know about the hazard — we would have fixed it."
The constructive notice doctrine eliminates this defense when the hazard existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it. A pothole that grew over months, cracked asphalt in a main pedestrian path, or a light that employees noted as out weeks before your fall all establish that the property owner should have known. We pull maintenance logs, inspection schedules, and prior incident reports to show the timeline.
Tactic 04
"The parking operator is responsible, not us."
When a property owner leases a lot to a parking management company, they often argue that all maintenance responsibility transferred to the operator. This is rarely a complete defense. Property owners typically retain structural maintenance duties even under management contracts, and both parties may share liability for surface defects. We obtain the lease agreement and management contract to show which party was responsible — and whether either one performed their duty.

Questions & answers

Parking lot slip & fall FAQ.

Liability in a parking lot fall typically falls on the property owner, the parking lot operator, or the management company responsible for maintaining the surface — or some combination of all three. Under California Civil Code § 1714, every person owes a duty of ordinary care in the management of their property. When a shopping center owner leases their lot to a parking management company, both parties may share liability: the owner for the underlying structural condition of the pavement, and the operator for day-to-day maintenance and inspection failures. We investigate lease agreements and maintenance contracts to identify every responsible party.
Common hazards that create liability include potholes and depressions in asphalt, cracked or heaved pavement sections, unmarked speed bumps, standing water that pools after rain, oil or fluid spills on driving lanes, inadequate lighting in structures and open lots, ADA non-compliance such as missing curb cuts or improperly sloped accessible routes, and wheel-stop placement that creates tripping hazards. The key question is not just whether the hazard existed, but whether the property owner knew or should have known about it through reasonable inspection and maintenance — and failed to fix or warn about it.
Yes. Under 28 CFR Part 36, commercial facilities must maintain accessible routes in operable condition, including parking lot surfaces, curb ramps, and accessible parking spaces. When a property owner fails to maintain ADA-compliant surfaces — such as allowing curb ramps to crack and heave, accessible routes to become uneven, or accessible spaces to lose required markings — that failure can be direct evidence of negligence. An ADA violation does not automatically create civil liability in a personal injury case, but it strongly supports the argument that the property owner knew the surface was defective and failed to correct it.
If your fall occurred in a city-owned parking lot, city-operated parking structure, or any government-controlled facility, Government Code § 911.2 requires you to file an administrative tort claim within six months of the date of injury — not two years. This is a hard deadline: missing it permanently bars your claim regardless of how severe your injuries are. The government tort claim must be filed with the specific agency responsible for the facility, which may be the City of Los Angeles Bureau of Engineering, the Los Angeles Department of Transportation, or another agency. Contact an attorney immediately if government property is involved.
Property owners are liable both when they created the hazardous condition and when they had constructive notice — meaning they should have known about it through regular inspection and maintenance. A pothole that has grown over months, cracked asphalt that workers and employees walked past repeatedly, or a lighting failure that was reported to management all create constructive notice. We obtain maintenance logs, inspection schedules, prior incident reports, and employee records to establish the timeline. Surveillance footage from the lot itself sometimes shows the hazard was present long before your fall — which is why we send preservation letters immediately.
Two years from the date of injury under California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 for claims against private property owners, operators, or management companies. Six months under Government Code § 911.2 if the lot or structure is government-owned or operated. But the practical deadline is much shorter: surveillance footage from the parking lot is typically overwritten in 24 to 72 hours, and the property owner will repair the hazard as soon as they learn of the injury claim.
