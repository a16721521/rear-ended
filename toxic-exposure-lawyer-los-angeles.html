Toxic exposure on property

When a hazard makes you sick, the landlord's knowledge is what makes the case.

Toxic exposure premises liability covers illness caused by a hazardous condition inside a building you don't own — mold and water intrusion in an apartment, lead-based paint flaking off a wall in a pre-1978 unit, asbestos disturbed during a renovation you never signed up for, carbon monoxide from a poorly vented water heater, or a chemical spill in an office break room. It is distinct from a workplace exposure claim against an employer (usually a workers' compensation matter) and from a product liability claim against a manufacturer. Here, the property owner or operator's failure to maintain safe conditions is the source of liability.

Every California property owner owes a duty of reasonable care to prevent foreseeable harm under Civil Code § 1714. For residential landlords, that duty is sharpened by Civil Code § 1941.1, which makes freedom from mold and water intrusion part of the implied warranty of habitability — a landlord who is put on notice of a leak or mold growth and fails to make a timely, adequate repair is in breach regardless of how the moisture originated.

Two additional statutes matter specifically in toxic exposure cases. Health & Safety Code § 17920.10 requires disclosure of known lead-based paint hazards in buildings constructed before 1978 — much of the older housing stock across Los Angeles. And Health & Safety Code § 13113.8 requires carbon monoxide detectors in most dwelling units with a fuel-burning appliance or attached garage; a landlord's failure to install or maintain a required detector is powerful evidence of negligence in a CO poisoning case. Renovation work that disturbs asbestos-containing materials in older LA buildings without proper abatement creates a separate, foreseeable exposure risk.

Medical causation is usually the hardest-fought issue in these cases — insurers routinely argue your symptoms could come from anywhere else. Building a case that survives that argument means environmental testing from an independent industrial hygienist and a medical toxicologist's opinion connecting the specific contaminant to your specific injury. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after discovering a toxic hazard.

Toxic exposure cases live and die on environmental testing and medical documentation. Both get harder to gather the longer you wait.

Free case review →
1
When you notice something's wrong
See a doctor and describe the possible exposure.

Toxic exposure symptoms — headaches, respiratory irritation, rashes, fatigue — often get treated as a routine illness. Tell your doctor specifically about the property: mold smell, peeling paint, a chemical odor, water intrusion, or a CO alarm going off. That detail belongs in the medical record from day one.

2
At the property
Photograph and document the hazard immediately.

Photograph visible mold, water stains, chipping or peeling paint, chemical residue, or a malfunctioning CO detector. Note the date, location, and extent of what you see. Don't clean up or remediate anything yourself before it's documented — the condition itself is evidence.

3
In writing
Send written notice to the landlord or property manager.

California habitability law requires that a landlord be given notice and a reasonable opportunity to fix mold and water intrusion. Put that notice in writing and keep a copy. An inadequate or absent response to a documented notice becomes evidence in itself.

4
Independent testing
Get independent environmental testing — don't rely on the landlord's own report.

An independent industrial hygienist can conduct air sampling, surface swabs, or paint chip lead testing whose results the property owner can't control. Landlord-commissioned testing is frequently timed, worded, or scoped to minimize findings.

5
Ongoing medical care
See the right medical specialists and follow through on treatment.

A medical toxicologist or occupational medicine physician can connect a specific exposure to specific symptoms — the single most contested issue in toxic exposure cases. Keep every appointment and follow every treatment plan; gaps in care get used against you.

6
Get an attorney involved
Preserve records before the property owner does damage control.

Prior tenant complaints, inspection reports, disclosure forms, maintenance logs, and code enforcement records can disappear once repairs begin. An attorney can send a preservation letter and request these records before renovation erases the evidence.

What toxic exposure causes

Exposure you can't see
causes damage you can feel.

Toxic exposure injuries often develop slowly and get dismissed as unrelated illness. Early medical documentation is what turns a vague symptom into a provable claim.

Respiratory Illness & Asthma
Mold spores and chemical fumes trigger and worsen asthma, chronic bronchitis, and other respiratory conditions. Children, older adults, and anyone with pre-existing lung disease are especially vulnerable to prolonged indoor exposure.
Carbon Monoxide Poisoning
A malfunctioning or improperly vented appliance without a functioning detector can produce dangerous CO levels with no warning. Poisoning ranges from headaches and confusion to permanent cognitive impairment and, in severe cases, death.
Lead Poisoning & Neurological Damage
Chipping or peeling lead paint in older buildings poses the greatest risk to young children, causing developmental delays, cognitive impairment, and behavioral problems. Adults can experience neurological symptoms and organ damage from prolonged exposure.
Asbestos-Related Disease
Asbestos fibers released during uninspected renovation or demolition work can cause mesothelioma, asbestosis, and lung cancer — diseases with a latency period that can stretch decades beyond the exposure itself.
Chemical Burns & Irritation
Contaminated water or an industrial chemical spill in a commercial building can cause chemical burns, severe skin and eye irritation, and respiratory damage requiring emergency treatment and specialized follow-up care.
Immune & Systemic Illness
Chronic exposure to mold and mycotoxins is increasingly linked to fatigue, cognitive fog, and autoimmune flare-ups — a multi-system illness that's difficult to diagnose without environmental testing that correlates conditions to symptoms.
What they don't want you to know

The landlord's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Property owners carry insurance and defense teams for exactly this scenario. Here's what they typically argue in toxic exposure cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"We didn't know about the hazard"
Landlords routinely claim ignorance to escape liability. Prior tenant complaints, work orders, and inspection reports often establish constructive notice — and for lead paint in pre-1978 buildings, the disclosure duty under Health & Safety Code § 17920.10 applies regardless of what the landlord claims to have known.
Tactic 02
"Your symptoms could be from anywhere else"
This is the most common defense in toxic exposure cases, because causation is genuinely difficult to prove. Independent environmental testing paired with a medical toxicologist's causation opinion connects the specific contaminant found on the property to your specific diagnosis — the combination insurers have the hardest time attacking.
Tactic 03
"The tenant caused the mold — bad ventilation, poor housekeeping"
Insurers try to shift blame for mold onto tenant behavior. Mold requires a moisture source, and identifying that source — a roof leak, plumbing failure, or inadequate waterproofing — is the landlord's structural responsibility under the implied warranty of habitability, regardless of how the unit was kept.
Tactic 04
"This building is exempt from disclosure requirements"
Some owners argue their building predates or falls outside a specific disclosure statute. The general duty of reasonable care under Civil Code § 1714 doesn't depend on a building's age or a disclosure exemption — and carbon monoxide detector requirements under Health & Safety Code § 13113.8 apply broadly to occupied dwelling units regardless.

Questions & answers

Toxic exposure FAQ.

Toxic exposure premises liability covers illness or injury caused by a hazardous condition on someone else's property — not a workplace chemical exposure claim (which usually falls under workers' compensation) and not a defective product claim. Common examples include mold and water intrusion in apartments, lead-based paint in pre-1978 buildings, asbestos disturbed during renovation, carbon monoxide from malfunctioning appliances or missing detectors, and contaminated water or chemical spills in residential or commercial buildings. The property owner or operator's failure to maintain safe conditions, disclose known hazards, or comply with California's habitability and safety statutes is what creates liability.
Liability generally falls on the property owner and, in commercial and multi-unit residential buildings, the property management company responsible for maintenance and inspections. Landlords are liable when they knew or should have known about a hazard — mold from an unrepaired leak, deteriorating lead paint, or asbestos disturbed during a renovation — and failed to remediate it or warn tenants. Contractors who perform renovation work without proper asbestos or lead abatement procedures can also be independently liable, as can prior owners who concealed a known hazard during a sale.
Civil Code § 1941.1 makes habitability — including freedom from mold and water intrusion — an implied condition of every California residential lease. A landlord who receives notice of mold or a water leak and fails to make a reasonable, timely repair is in breach of that duty and can be held liable for resulting health problems, property damage, and, in cases involving concealment, punitive damages. This duty exists independent of the general duty of reasonable care that all property owners owe under Civil Code § 1714.
For any residential building constructed before 1978, California's lead-based paint disclosure rule under Health & Safety Code § 17920.10 requires landlords to disclose known lead hazards to tenants. Separately, when a building undergoes renovation or demolition, contractors and owners have duties to identify and properly abate asbestos-containing materials rather than disturb them, since airborne asbestos fibers from uninspected renovation work create a foreseeable and often unknown exposure risk to occupants.
Medical causation is usually the most contested issue in these cases. The strongest evidence combines environmental testing — air sampling, surface swabs, or paint chip analysis performed by an independent industrial hygienist — with a medical toxicologist's opinion connecting the specific contaminant and exposure level to your specific symptoms. Prior tenant complaints, inspection reports, maintenance records, and code enforcement history help establish that the property owner knew or should have known about the hazard before you were exposed.
California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years from the date of injury to file a personal injury lawsuit. But toxic exposure injuries are often latent — asbestos-related disease, for example, can take decades to appear — so California's discovery rule can extend the filing deadline to two years from when you discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, that your illness was caused by the exposure. If a government-owned building is involved, you have only six months under Government Code § 911.2 to file an administrative claim. Because discovery-rule cases turn on what you knew and when, documenting the timeline early with a qualified attorney is critical.
