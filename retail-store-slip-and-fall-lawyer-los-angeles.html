Retail premises liability

The store's own business model put you at risk. The inspection logs tell us how long.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of ordinary care on every property owner — and for a retail store, that duty is active, not passive. A grocery chain, big box retailer, or pharmacy that invites hundreds of customers per hour to handle produce, beverages, and refrigerated merchandise creates a continuous and foreseeable risk of floor hazards. The duty of care demands a maintenance program equal to that risk: regular aisle inspections, documented sweep schedules, and prompt response when hazards are reported. When that program fails — or when the records show it was never consistently followed — the store is liable for the injuries that result.

The critical legal question in a retail store slip and fall is notice: did the store know, or should it have known, about the hazardous condition? Actual notice is straightforward — an employee saw the spill. Constructive notice is more common and more powerful: the condition existed long enough that routine inspection would have revealed it. A produce aisle spill present for 40 minutes, condensation accumulating under a refrigerator case for weeks, or a mat that had been bunching at the entrance for days — these are constructive notice situations. When a retailer's self-service business model itself creates the foreseeable conditions that caused your fall, that foreseeability bears on whether the store's inspection schedule was adequate given what it knew its model produced.

Big box retailers and grocery chains have corporate legal and claims teams that activate as soon as an incident report is filed. They preserve evidence in their favor and often move to minimize the corporate record before any claim is made. The sweep logs, CCTV footage, maintenance tickets, and prior incident reports that prove liability have short lifespans — CCTV footage is typically overwritten in 24 to 72 hours. Slip and fall vs. trip and fall distinctions matter at trial: a slip involves loss of traction on a hazardous surface, while a trip involves catching a foot on a physical obstruction. Both carry the same duty of care analysis under Civil Code § 1714, but each calls for different evidence.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by your own percentage of fault — it is never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after a retail store fall.

Retail chains have corporate claims teams that begin working the moment an incident report is filed. Surveillance footage disappears in 24–72 hours. Here is how to protect your claim before the store gets ahead of the evidence.

1
At the scene
Report the fall to the store manager and insist on an incident report.

Find the manager on duty and tell them you fell. Ask them to complete an incident report before you leave — and ask for a copy. Large retailers have standardized corporate incident report procedures; the document will record the date, time, location, and the condition described by employees. This is one piece of evidence the store controls and you do not. Do not say you are "fine" or "okay." You do not yet know the full extent of your injuries, and any statement minimizing pain can be used against you later.

2
At the scene
Photograph the hazard, any signage — and the absence of signage.

Photograph the floor condition that caused your fall: the spill, the leaking refrigeration unit, the bunched mat, the merchandise in the aisle. If there is no wet floor cone or warning sign, photograph that too — the absence of signage at the time of the fall is important evidence. Note the exact location, the time, and any overhead surveillance cameras you can see. Take multiple angles — close up on the hazard and wide shots showing the surrounding area and any nearby employees. Retail stores clean up hazards within minutes of a fall report.

3
At the scene
Get witness names and contact information before they leave.

Other shoppers who saw you fall, saw the hazard before you arrived, or saw employees walk past the spill without addressing it are potential witnesses. Get names and phone numbers before they leave the store — once people disperse, locating them becomes very difficult. Fellow shoppers who can describe the condition of the aisle before your fall, or who can testify that no employee was monitoring the area, provide independent evidence of constructive notice that the store cannot dispute.

4
Same day
See a doctor — do not wait until the next day.

Hip fractures, spinal injuries, and traumatic brain injuries from falls do not always present with immediate severe pain. Adrenaline at the scene masks symptoms. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this specific fall, in this specific store, on this specific date. A gap in treatment — even 24 to 48 hours — becomes an argument that the fall was not the true cause of your injuries. Emergency rooms and urgent care clinics document the mechanism of injury in the chart; make sure the cause of the fall is accurately recorded.

5
Same day
Preserve your footwear and clothing exactly as they are.

Your shoes are evidence. Retailers and their insurers routinely argue that your footwear was inappropriate for the floor conditions — open-toed shoes, sandals, high heels, or worn soles become comparative fault arguments. Do not wash, clean, or discard your shoes. Bag them and set them aside. If possible, photograph the soles at the scene before removing them. The condition of your shoe's heel and sole at the time of the fall can rebut a footwear defense entirely if documented contemporaneously.

6
As soon as possible
Call Law Dog before speaking with the store's insurance adjuster.

The retailer's insurer will contact you quickly — often within 24 to 48 hours — presenting the call as a routine information gathering step. Their goal is a recorded statement, made before you understand your rights, that can be used to shift fault and minimize the claim. Do not give one. Do not sign any document the store presents. What retail store falls cause

A fall on a hard retail floor
is never trivial.

Hard tile, concrete, and polished floors in retail environments transmit fall forces with no cushion. The injuries reflect that contact. Insurers minimize them. We document every consequence.

Hip Fractures
The most serious and common severe injury from retail store falls, particularly in older adults. A sideways fall onto hard tile or concrete transmits force directly to the hip. Displaced fractures require total hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation — with significant risk of secondary complications that are well-documented and life-altering.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Head contact with a hard retail floor during a backward or sideways fall can cause concussion, skull fracture, subdural hematoma, or diffuse axonal injury. Cognitive impairment, memory loss, and personality changes may not appear for days after the fall — making same-day medical evaluation essential even when the victim initially appears uninjured and declines transport.
Spinal Injuries
A backward fall on a wet floor compresses the lumbar spine on impact, causing herniated discs, vertebral fractures, or spinal cord damage with partial or permanent neurological consequences. Adrenaline frequently masks the severity at the scene — victims who walked out of the store have later required lumbar fusion surgery when symptoms fully emerged over the following days and weeks.
Broken Wrists
The instinct to break a fall with outstretched hands causes wrist fractures — often comminuted fractures requiring open reduction and internal fixation with surgical hardware. Grip strength and fine motor function may be permanently reduced, affecting computer work, manual labor, and basic daily tasks indefinitely after treatment ends.
Knee & Shoulder Injuries
Twisting during a slip or catching on a display during a trip can tear the ACL, meniscus, or rotator cuff. These injuries require surgical repair and extended physical therapy, with variable long-term outcomes. Full functional recovery is not guaranteed — many victims have lasting instability, chronic pain, and activity restrictions that affect employment and quality of life long after clinical treatment ends.
Ankle Fractures & Soft Tissue
Slipping on a wet retail floor or tripping on a mat commonly causes ankle fractures with ligament involvement, sprains, and tendon tears that insurers aggressively minimize. These injuries cause lasting joint instability and chronic pain that affects daily function. They do not always appear on standard X-rays — MRI is frequently required to fully document the injury and build the damages case.
What they don't want you to know

The retailer's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Grocery chains and big box retailers carry large commercial liability policies and experienced corporate defense teams. Here is what they typically argue in retail store slip and fall cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The spill just happened — we had no time to respond."
This is the most common and most contested argument in retail store slip and fall cases. The store claims the hazard appeared moments before your fall, eliminating any notice obligation. Sweep logs and CCTV footage tell the real story. If the store cannot produce a documented inspection of the area within a reasonable time before your fall, constructive notice is established. And when a store's own self-service model — open produce sections, beverage aisles, salad bars — creates continuous spill conditions, a claim of total surprise becomes very difficult to sustain.
Tactic 02
"Our sweep log shows the aisle was inspected 10 minutes before you fell."
Retail sweep logs are created by store employees and are self-serving documents. We scrutinize them carefully — checking whether the times recorded are consistent with employee shift records, whether the same handwriting fills every line for an entire shift, and whether the claimed inspection intervals are physically possible given the number of aisles and employees on duty. When sweep log entries don't hold up, they become evidence of falsification — which in the right case can support a claim for punitive damages beyond compensatory recovery.
Tactic 03
"You weren't watching where you were going."
This is a comparative fault argument, not a complete defense. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault — not eliminated. And the argument only carries weight if the hazard was actually visible and avoidable. A clear liquid on a light-colored floor, a spill partially obscured by a shopping cart, or a mat that looks flat but is folded at the edge is not something a shopper browsing merchandise is expected to detect before falling. We build the evidence around the hazard's visibility and the store's failure to address it.
Tactic 04
"Our corporate incident report confirms our employees followed procedure."
Corporate incident reports are prepared by store employees, often with awareness that they will be used in litigation. They frequently minimize the hazard description, overstate how quickly cleanup occurred, and omit any admission of how long the condition existed. The incident report is a starting point — not the end of the inquiry. We obtain independent evidence: CCTV footage of the area before your fall, statements from employee witnesses, prior incident reports at the same location, and maintenance records showing whether refrigeration units in the area had been flagged for leaks before the day of your accident.

Retail store slip & fall FAQ.

Retail store slip and fall accidents stem from many sources: produce aisle spills that go uninspected, condensation pooling beneath refrigeration and freezer units, freshly mopped floors reopened to customers before they are dry, merchandise placed in walkways, loose or bunched entryway mats, and spilled beverages in self-checkout areas. Big box retailers and grocery chains see these incidents at high rates because their self-service business model puts customers in direct contact with liquids and unstable merchandise at all times. California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of ordinary care on every property owner — and for a retailer whose store model creates foreseeable spill conditions, that duty requires active inspection and maintenance, not just reactive cleanup after someone is injured.
The mode of operation doctrine is a legal theory that holds a retailer on constructive notice of foreseeable hazards created by its own business model — even when there is no direct evidence of how long a specific spill was present. When a store operates a self-service produce section, an open salad bar, or a beverage aisle where customers routinely handle liquids, courts recognize that the store's operating model itself creates a continuous and foreseeable risk of floor hazards. In California, mode of operation evidence is relevant to the notice inquiry — it shows that the hazard was a predictable consequence of the store's own practices and not a random or isolated event. Combined with the store's failure to produce adequate inspection logs or sweep records, mode of operation evidence significantly strengthens a constructive notice argument. We use it to challenge the retailer's claim that they simply didn't know — because knowing was part of their job.

Corporate incident reports filed by store employees at the time of the fall often contain admissions about the condition.
