Construction zone premises liability

Three defendants. One fall. Your right to full recovery.

Construction zones in Los Angeles create some of the most complex slip and fall cases — not because the hazards are subtle, but because responsibility is deliberately fragmented. General contractors, subcontractors, and property owners each have distinct legal duties, and each will point the finger at the others when a visitor or pedestrian is injured. California law cuts through that defense. Under Civil Code § 1714, every person — including property owners and contractors — owes a duty of ordinary care in the management of their property and the conduct of their work.

Cal/OSHA's construction safety regulations under Title 8 of the California Code of Regulations set specific requirements for protecting the public in and around active construction zones. Contractors must install and maintain adequate pedestrian barriers, provide properly lit and surfaced temporary walkways when existing paths are obstructed, and protect against overhead hazards such as scaffolding debris and tools dropped from elevation. When a contractor skips barricades to save time, routes visitors through an uneven temporary surface without adequate warning, or leaves debris in a public walkway, those violations are direct evidence of negligence under California law.

California also recognizes the "peculiar risk" doctrine — a rule that prevents property owners from escaping liability by pointing to the independent contractor they hired. When a property owner commissions work that poses a peculiar risk of harm to third parties — which includes virtually any active construction adjacent to occupied buildings or public walkways — the property owner cannot delegate that responsibility away. The California Supreme Court has consistently held that landowners who benefit from construction projects remain liable to third parties injured as a result of that work, even when the contractor controlled the specific operation that caused the injury.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — never eliminated. The standard deadline is two years under CCP § 335.1. If the project was government-funded or on government property, you have just six months under Government Code § 911.2. Step-by-step

What to do after a construction zone fall.

Construction projects involve contractors with legal teams and insurers who activate immediately after an incident. The permits, safety plans, and surveillance footage that prove negligence can vanish as construction moves on. Act fast.

1
At the scene
Call 911 and report the fall to the site supervisor.

Get the responding officer's name and report number if police respond. Report the fall directly to the general contractor's site superintendent or foreman — California law requires contractors to document workplace and site incidents. Get the name of the contractor, the construction company, and the property owner if possible. Ask for a copy of any incident report before you leave the area.

2
At the scene
Photograph the hazardous condition before it changes.

Document the specific condition that caused your fall: the temporary walkway surface, missing or inadequate barriers, the debris field, low-hanging scaffolding, uneven transition surfaces, or lack of signage. Construction sites change hour by hour — the hazard that caused your fall may be remedied, removed, or built over within the same workday. Photograph surrounding conditions, any posted warnings or the absence of them, and note the time, exact location, and names of any witnesses on site.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved before the contractor's team takes over the scene.

General contractors and their insurers dispatch claims representatives quickly — sometimes within hours of a serious incident. Their job is to document the scene in the contractor's favor, not yours. An attorney can send preservation letters immediately demanding that all parties retain building permits, site safety plans, Cal/OSHA inspection logs, incident reports, surveillance footage, and the physical evidence at the location of your fall. This spoliation hold is the single most time-sensitive step in a construction zone case.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical evaluation and follow through completely.

Falls on construction sites often involve significant force — drops from elevation, impacts with debris, or violent falls on hard unfinished surfaces. Even if you walked away from the scene, traumatic brain injuries, internal injuries, and spinal compression can present with delayed symptoms. A same-day evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this specific fall. Every subsequent treatment, specialist referral, and physical therapy visit builds the damages case.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer or contractor.

The general contractor's insurer, the subcontractor's insurer, and the property owner's insurer will each contact you — sometimes framing their calls as routine paperwork. Their goal is to get a recorded statement that can be used to place fault on you: suggesting you ignored warning signs, entered a restricted area, or weren't watching where you were walking. Let your attorney handle all communication with every insurer. You are entitled to legal representation before providing any statement.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document every financial loss.

Construction zone injuries often require extended treatment — surgeries, rehabilitation, and specialist follow-ups over months or years. Every medical visit, prescription, therapy session, and day of missed work builds the damages case. Keep receipts for transportation to medical appointments, out-of-pocket medical costs, and any home care assistance your injuries require. Share everything with your attorney as it comes in — do not wait until you think treatment is complete.

What construction zone falls cause

A fall in an active construction zone
is rarely minor.

Uneven temporary surfaces, sudden elevation changes, hard unfinished floors, and falling debris make construction zone falls more severe than typical premises incidents. The injuries reflect that.

Hip & Pelvic Fractures
Falls on hard unfinished concrete or through temporary flooring transmit tremendous impact force to the hip and pelvis. Displaced hip fractures often require total hip replacement, weeks of hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation — with significant risk of long-term complications that affect mobility for life.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Head contact with a concrete floor, steel beam, or scaffolding component during a construction zone fall can cause concussion, skull fracture, or intracranial hemorrhage. Debris falling from elevation can strike without warning. Cognitive changes, memory problems, and personality shifts may not become fully apparent for days or weeks after the impact.
Spinal Cord Injuries
Falls from elevation — through an unprotected floor opening, off an unstable temporary platform, or over an unmarked edge — transmit compression forces directly through the spine. Vertebral fractures, disc herniation, and spinal cord damage can produce partial or complete paralysis with lasting neurological consequences that require lifelong care and adaptive equipment.
Knee & Ankle Fractures
Tripping on uneven temporary surfaces, stepping into a debris pile, or stepping onto an unstable surface causes violent twisting and impact forces at the knee and ankle. ACL and meniscus tears require surgical reconstruction; comminuted ankle fractures often need hardware and produce chronic instability that affects mobility and employment capacity permanently.
Lacerations & Puncture Wounds
Exposed rebar, protruding screws, broken concrete edges, and scattered construction debris create laceration and puncture hazards that would not exist on a properly maintained and barricaded construction site. Deep puncture wounds from metal debris carry infection risk, and lacerations from rebar or broken materials can cause permanent scarring and nerve damage.
Toxic Exposure Injuries
Active construction generates hazardous dust and fumes — silica dust from concrete cutting, asbestos from demolition of older structures, and chemical off-gassing from adhesives, solvents, and coatings. Pedestrians routed through or adjacent to active work areas without proper respiratory protection can suffer acute respiratory injury and long-term lung damage from a single exposure.
What they don't want you to know

The contractor's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

General contractors carry large commercial liability policies and experienced defense teams. Here is what they typically argue in construction zone fall cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"You entered a restricted construction area — you assumed the risk"
The assumption of risk doctrine has very limited application in California premises liability cases. A visitor who uses a route that appears open, or who has no clear alternative path, does not assume the risk of contractor negligence simply by walking through a construction-adjacent area. If the contractor failed to provide adequate barriers, clear signage, or an alternative route, the risk was created by the contractor — not assumed by you.
Tactic 02
"The hazard was clearly marked. You should have seen it."
A single orange cone, a piece of tape, or a generic "Construction Ahead" sign does not satisfy Cal/OSHA's requirements for pedestrian protection in an active construction zone. California's regulations require specific barrier types, minimum widths for temporary walkways, overhead protection where overhead hazards exist, and adequate lighting. Minimal or inadequate markings that do not meet the regulatory standard are evidence of negligence — not a defense against it.
Tactic 03
"The subcontractor who created that hazard is responsible — not us"
The general contractor is responsible for overall site safety under California law — including the work performed by every subcontractor it hires. And the property owner remains liable under the peculiar risk doctrine regardless of which contractor controlled the specific area. Finger-pointing between the GC and its subs is a litigation tactic designed to delay resolution and confuse juries, not an accurate statement of California liability law. We name all parties and establish each one's independent duty.
Tactic 04
"You should have been watching where you were going in an obvious work zone"
California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2) reduces but never eliminates your recovery even if you bore some fault. The fact that construction was visible does not mean the specific hazard that caused your fall was obvious or adequately warned against — a busy construction environment is precisely the kind of setting where the contractor's duty to provide clear, adequate, and properly maintained pedestrian protection is at its highest.

Questions & answers

Construction zone slip & fall FAQ.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of ordinary care on every person — including property owners, general contractors, and subcontractors — in the management of their property and the conduct of their work. In a construction zone, this duty is heightened by Cal/OSHA's construction safety regulations under Title 8 of the California Code of Regulations, which require that pedestrian walkways be clearly marked and maintained, overhead hazards be protected by barricades, and construction areas be properly segregated from public traffic. When any of these requirements are violated and a pedestrian or building visitor is injured, that violation is direct evidence of negligence.
Construction zone injuries typically involve multiple defendants: the general contractor, who has overall responsibility for site safety and coordination of all subcontractors; individual subcontractors, who controlled the specific area where you were injured; and the property owner, who remains liable even after hiring a contractor under California's "peculiar risk" doctrine. The peculiar risk doctrine prevents property owners from delegating liability for work that poses special dangers to third parties. In practice, this means you may have claims against all three parties simultaneously — each with different insurance policies and different evidence supporting liability.
Building permits and site safety plans are the first category of evidence — they establish what the contractor was authorized to do and what safety measures were required. Cal/OSHA inspection records and any citations issued for the project reveal whether the contractor was already on notice of safety violations. Site superintendent incident logs document prior complaints or near-misses that establish the contractor knew about the hazardous condition. Surveillance footage from the construction site or adjacent businesses can show the state of the walkway before your fall. Physical evidence — the temporary walkway surface, barrier placement, and signage — disappears quickly as construction progresses, making immediate attorney involvement critical.
