Commercial vehicle accidents

Multiple defendants. Maximum coverage. Our job.

A truck accident case is nothing like a car accident case. The defendants include the truck driver, the motor carrier, the shipper, the cargo loader, the vehicle manufacturer, and sometimes a maintenance contractor. Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) regulations impose duties on each party that are separate from California tort law. A successful truck injury case requires understanding both.

Commercial carriers are required by federal law to carry minimum insurance — $750,000 for general freight, up to $5 million for hazardous materials. That sounds like a lot. But catastrophic truck crash injuries regularly exceed those limits, and carriers have experienced in-house claims teams and outside counsel working the case from the moment the crash is reported. They are investigating before you leave the hospital.

The most important evidence — the electronic logging device (ELD), the event data recorder (black box), driver qualification files, hours-of-service logs, maintenance records, and dashcam footage — is all controlled by the carrier. Carriers are not required to preserve this data indefinitely. Without a prompt litigation hold letter, critical evidence can legally be overwritten or destroyed within 30 to 180 days.

Law Dog handles commercial vehicle crash cases on full contingency. We issue preservation demands the day you call us, retain accident reconstructionists and trucking regulations experts, and litigate against carriers and their insurers without hesitation. Under pure comparative fault (Civil Code § 1431.2), you can recover even if you contributed to the crash. If a government vehicle was involved, Gov. Code § 911.2 gives you just six months. Immediately after

What to do after a truck crash.

The carrier's claims team is notified the moment the crash enters their system. They investigate immediately. The steps you take in the first 24 hours are critical to matching their effort.

At the scene — immediate
Get the truck's DOT number, plate, and carrier name

The DOT number on the truck's door panel links to the carrier's entire federal safety record — inspection history, out-of-service orders, violation history. Photograph it clearly. Also get the driver's CDL, insurance certificate, and bill of lading if they will show it.

2
At the scene — immediate
Photograph the truck's position, configuration, and damage

The truck's final position, the trailer angle, skid marks, road conditions, and any visible damage to the cargo or trailer. The impact pattern on the truck and your vehicle can tell an accident reconstructionist exactly how the crash occurred.

3
At the scene — critical
Ensure a full commercial vehicle crash report is created

Commercial vehicle crashes have separate reporting requirements. The truck driver must undergo mandatory drug and alcohol testing after a serious crash. Make sure this is documented. Get the responding officer's name, badge number, and report number before you leave.

Truck crash injuries are severe. Spinal compression, internal bleeding, and TBI may not be immediately symptomatic. A same-day ER visit is essential — both for your health and to establish causation. A gap in treatment becomes an argument that your injuries aren't serious.

What we document and fight for

Truck crashes produce
catastrophic injuries.

The weight differential between a loaded semi and a passenger car is 30:1. At highway speeds, the physics are unforgiving. We retain medical and biomechanical experts who document every consequence.

Spinal Cord Injuries
Compression or shear forces from high-speed impact can cause partial or complete paralysis. Even incomplete cord injuries produce partial loss of function with unpredictable recovery trajectories and significant long-term care costs.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Violent deceleration and impact against interior vehicle surfaces causes diffuse axonal injury, hemorrhage, and contusions. Cognitive, personality, and memory consequences can be permanent and worsen over time.
Internal Organ Damage
Blunt abdominal trauma from steering wheel impact, seatbelt forces, or vehicle intrusion can rupture the liver, spleen, kidneys, or bowel. Internal bleeding is not always immediately apparent — same-day imaging is essential.
Crush Injuries
Vehicle intrusion from the truck pushing into the passenger compartment causes structural compression of limbs, pelvis, and torso. Compartment syndrome and vascular injury are common complications requiring emergency intervention.
Multiple Fractures
Pelvis, femur, humerus, and rib fractures from high-force impact frequently require surgical fixation, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation. Multiple simultaneous fractures significantly increase case value.
Amputations
Crush injuries and tissue devascularization from compartment syndrome can necessitate amputation of extremities. Prosthetics, adaptive equipment, vocational retraining, and home modification are all compensable.
Burns
Fuel ignition from post-crash fire causes severe thermal burns requiring intensive care, multiple skin grafting surgeries, and reconstructive procedures. Permanent disfigurement is common and significantly increases non-economic damages.
Soft Tissue Damage
Ligament, tendon, and muscle injuries from violent deceleration. Chronic pain and functional limitation from severe soft tissue damage are commonly undervalued by insurers — we document them with specialist testimony.
Psychological Trauma
PTSD, driving phobia, chronic anxiety, and depression following a catastrophic truck crash are real, documented, and fully compensable injuries. We retain psychiatric expert evaluation to establish and quantify these damages.
Carrier defense playbook exposed

What every trucking company
lawyer is trained to argue.

Commercial carriers and their insurers maintain experienced claims and litigation teams. They know every defense argument and every way to delay, minimize, and complicate your case. We have faced each one — and we know how to counter them.

Tactic 01
"Our driver was compliant with all federal regulations."
FMCSA compliance is the floor, not a defense. A driver can be within hours-of-service limits and still be dangerously fatigued. A truck can pass its last inspection and still have failing brakes. Federal regulatory compliance does not satisfy California's reasonable care standard — it is a minimum baseline. We investigate what actually happened, not just what the logs say.
Tactic 02
"You were in the truck's blind spot — the driver couldn't see you."
Commercial trucks are required to have functioning mirrors that minimize blind spots. Carriers are required to train drivers on blind spot management. A driver who proceeds through a lane change, turn, or merge without accounting for blind spots is negligent. Blind spot is not a legal defense — it is a driver training failure.
Tactic 03
"Our electronic logs show the driver was within hours-of-service limits."
ELD data is the starting point, not the end of the analysis. We examine patterns of maximum-hour scheduling, whether rest periods were actually restful, and whether the carrier used scheduling practices to extend effective trip duration beyond legal limits. We also look for ELD tampering, which is illegal but documented in FMCSA enforcement data.
Tactic 04
"Your injuries were pre-existing."
The eggshell plaintiff rule applies to truck crash victims as it does to all personal injury plaintiffs. A prior back condition, previous neck injury, or reduced bone density from age does not limit the carrier's liability. The carrier is responsible for the full extent of the harm their driver caused, including aggravation of every pre-existing condition.
Tactic 05
"You were speeding / following too closely / changed lanes improperly."
Comparative fault arguments are standard defense tactics designed to shift liability. Under California's pure comparative fault rule, your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault — but not eliminated. When a 40-ton truck with a documented maintenance violation or a fatigue-impaired driver is involved, assigning meaningful fault to the smaller vehicle requires strong evidence that we counter with accident reconstruction.
Tactic 06
"Here's a settlement offer — let's resolve this quickly."
Quick settlement offers in truck crash cases are almost always dramatically lower than the true case value. Catastrophic injuries have long tails — follow-up surgeries, chronic pain management, lost earning capacity, and life-care costs. Once you sign a release, the case is permanently closed. Carriers make early offers specifically because victims haven't yet understood the full scope of their injuries.

Frequently asked

Questions about truck accident claims.

Multiple parties may be liable: the truck driver, the motor carrier (company that owns or operates the truck), the shipper that loaded or dispatched the cargo, a cargo loader if improper loading caused the crash, the vehicle or parts manufacturer if a defect contributed, and a maintenance contractor if negligent maintenance caused a failure. California allows all potentially liable parties to be named simultaneously. The depth of commercial carrier insurance — $750,000 to $5 million or more — makes identifying all defendants critically important.
The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration sets mandatory standards for commercial trucking: hours-of-service limits, mandatory rest periods, pre-trip inspection requirements, drug and alcohol testing, driver qualification standards, and vehicle maintenance schedules. When a carrier or driver violates these regulations and those violations contribute to a crash, the violation itself is evidence of negligence. FMCSA compliance records are public — we pull them immediately and compare them against the specific driver and carrier involved in your crash.
Federal law requires most commercial trucks to use ELDs that automatically record driving time, engine hours, vehicle movement, and miles driven. ELD data can prove a driver exceeded hours-of-service limits, drove without required rest, or that the carrier scheduled the driver in violation of federal law. This data has limited retention windows — often 30 to 180 days. We issue legal preservation demands the day you hire us. Once the data is overwritten, it is gone permanently.

Carriers frequently argue that their drivers are independent contractors to shield themselves from liability. California courts apply strict standards when evaluating this claim in the trucking context. Under California law, carriers who control the manner and means of a driver's work — route, schedule, cargo, equipment — are typically treated as employers for liability purposes. We investigate the true nature of the carrier-driver relationship and challenge independent contractor claims when the facts support it.

We identify every available insurance layer and pursue them all.
