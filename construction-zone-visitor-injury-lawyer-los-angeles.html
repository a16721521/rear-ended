Construction zone visitor injuries

Someone else's job site. Not your risk to bear.

Not every construction site injury involves a worker, and not every construction site injury involves the ground underfoot. A customer visiting a business undergoing renovation, a resident in a building being remodeled, or a pedestrian walking past a site with inadequate perimeter protection can be struck by falling material, injured by an unsecured piece of equipment, or hurt by a structural hazard that has nothing to do with tripping or slipping. California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of ordinary care on every person managing property or directing work — a duty that does not evaporate because the injured person wasn't on the payroll.

California also recognizes the "peculiar risk" doctrine, which prevents a property owner from escaping liability simply by pointing to the independent contractor they hired. Construction work is, by its nature, inherently dangerous to people nearby — and when a property owner commissions that work, the owner cannot fully delegate away responsibility for keeping visitors safe. Courts have applied this doctrine consistently to hold owners accountable when they fail to require or confirm adequate safety precautions, even though a contractor controlled the daily operations. It exists precisely so an owner can't use a contractor as a liability shield.

Cal/OSHA's site safety regulations set specific standards for barricading active hazards, securing scaffolding and equipment, protecting uncovered excavations, and guarding exposed rebar and other protruding hardware near public areas. When those standards are ignored — a single strand of caution tape where a rigid barrier was required, scaffolding left unsecured overnight, an excavation left open without covers or fencing — it is direct evidence of negligence. Liability in these cases is layered: the subcontractor who created the specific hazard, the general contractor responsible for overall site safety, and the property owner under the peculiar risk doctrine can all bear responsibility at once.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by fault attributable to you — never eliminated. The standard deadline is two years under CCP § 335.1; six months if the project was government-owned or -funded under Government Code § 911.2. Step-by-step

What to do after a construction zone injury.

The owner, general contractor, and subs each have insurers that activate fast. The barricade records and safety plans that prove negligence can disappear as the project moves on. Act quickly.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Call 911 and report the incident to the site supervisor.

Get the responding officer's name and report number if police or paramedics respond. Report what happened directly to the general contractor's site superintendent or foreman, and ask for the name of the general contractor, the subcontractor working in the area, and the property owner if you can get it. Request a copy of any incident report before you leave.

2
At the scene
Photograph the hazard before the site changes.

Document the specific condition that caused your injury: the falling material or where it fell from, the unsecured scaffolding or equipment, the exposed rebar or hardware, the uncovered excavation, or the missing or inadequate barricade. Construction sites are cleaned up and rearranged constantly — the exact hazard that hurt you may be gone within hours. Photograph the surrounding perimeter, any posted warnings or their absence, and note the time and names of witnesses.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved before the contractor's team controls the narrative.

General contractors, subcontractors, and property owners each dispatch claims representatives quickly after a serious incident — sometimes within hours. Their job is to document the scene favorably to their client, not to you. An attorney can send preservation letters immediately, demanding that every party retain site safety plans, barricade placement logs, Cal/OSHA inspection records, subcontractor agreements, and incident reports before anything is altered, lost, or "misplaced."

4
Same day
Get emergency medical evaluation and follow through completely.

Falling material, unsecured equipment, and structural hazards can cause serious impact and puncture injuries that are not immediately obvious. Even if you feel able to walk away, internal injuries, head trauma, and deep puncture wounds can worsen or become infected without prompt evaluation. A same-day medical record creates the causation link between your injuries and this specific incident.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer or contractor.

The general contractor's insurer, the responsible subcontractor's insurer, and the property owner's insurer may each contact you, sometimes framing it as routine paperwork. Their goal is to get a statement suggesting you wandered into a restricted area, ignored a sign, or weren't paying attention. Let your attorney handle every communication with every insurer before you say anything on the record.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document every financial loss.

Injuries from falling objects, unsecured equipment, or structural hazards can require surgery, wound care, and extended rehabilitation over months. Every medical visit, prescription, and day of missed work builds the damages case. Keep receipts for transportation, out-of-pocket costs, and any home care your recovery requires, and share everything with your attorney as it happens.

What construction zone hazards cause

You weren't on the job.
The site still hurt you.

Falling material, unsecured equipment, and exposed structural hazards produce forceful, often unpredictable injuries. The severity reflects that these are dangers a properly run site is supposed to prevent from ever reaching the public.

Traumatic Brain Injury
A tool, material, or piece of debris falling from scaffolding or an upper floor can strike a visitor without warning. Even a glancing impact can cause concussion, skull fracture, or intracranial hemorrhage, with cognitive and personality changes that may not fully surface for days or weeks.
Crush Injuries
Unsecured scaffolding sections, stacked materials, or heavy equipment that shifts or collapses can trap or crush a bystander. These injuries frequently require emergency surgery, extended hospitalization, and carry a heightened risk of compartment syndrome if not treated immediately.
Lacerations & Puncture Wounds
Exposed rebar, protruding screws or bolts, and sharp construction debris left unguarded near a walkway create serious laceration and impalement hazards. Deep puncture wounds from metal carry significant infection risk and can cause permanent nerve or tendon damage.
Spinal Cord Injuries
Direct impact from a falling object, or a fall into an uncovered excavation, can transmit compressive force through the spine. Vertebral fractures and spinal cord damage can result in partial or complete paralysis requiring lifelong care and adaptive equipment.
Fractures
Impacts from falling material, contact with unsecured equipment, or falls caused by inadequate barricades around excavations and hazards produce fractures across the body — from wrist and forearm breaks bracing for impact to more severe pelvic and femur fractures requiring surgical repair.
Eye & Respiratory Injuries
Flying debris, concrete or drywall dust, and particulate matter from active demolition or cutting can cause corneal injury, permanent vision impairment, and acute respiratory irritation in visitors who were never given protective equipment or adequate distance from the work.
What they don't want you to know

The contractor's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Owners, general contractors, and subs each carry commercial liability coverage and experienced defense teams. Here is what they typically argue in visitor injury cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"We hired an independent contractor — we're not liable for their work"
This is the exact argument California's peculiar risk doctrine was created to defeat. A property owner who commissions inherently dangerous work cannot fully delegate away responsibility for the safety of visitors, customers, and neighbors simply by hiring someone else to do it. If the owner failed to require or confirm adequate precautions, that liability doesn't disappear because a contractor was on site.
Tactic 02
"The area was barricaded and clearly marked"
A strip of caution tape is not a barricade. Cal/OSHA site safety regulations require specific rigid barrier types, minimum standoff distances, and overhead protection for active hazards — standards a single line of tape or a generic sign does not come close to meeting. Inadequate markings are evidence of negligence, not a defense against it.
Tactic 03
"The subcontractor who left the hazard is responsible — not us"
The general contractor is responsible for overall site safety under California law, including work performed by every subcontractor it hires and coordinates. The property owner remains independently liable under the peculiar risk doctrine regardless of which subcontractor's crew created the specific hazard. We name every responsible party rather than letting them point at each other.
Tactic 04
"You shouldn't have been anywhere near an active construction site"
If you were a customer, resident, or pedestrian using a route the business, building, or public sidewalk allowed, you were not trespassing. Under Civil Code § 1431.2's pure comparative fault rule, even partial fault only reduces your recovery — it never eliminates it. The duty to keep that route safe from active construction hazards belonged to the owner and contractor, not to you.

Construction zone visitor injury FAQ.

A slip and fall claim involves ground-surface hazards — an uneven temporary walkway, debris underfoot, a missing warning sign. A visitor injury claim covers the broader range of active construction dangers that don't involve the ground you're walking on: falling tools or materials, an unsecured scaffolding section, exposed rebar you didn't trip over but were cut by, an uncovered excavation, or a structural hazard from work in progress. Both are premises liability claims under Civil Code § 1714, but the evidence and the specific safety violations at issue are different. Many incidents involve elements of both, and we evaluate every angle.
Liability in a non-worker construction injury case typically spans three layers. The subcontractor whose crew created the specific hazard — unsecured scaffolding, an unbarricaded excavation, improperly stored materials — bears direct responsibility for that condition. The general contractor is responsible for overall site safety and coordination, including the duty to ensure every subcontractor's work doesn't endanger the public. And the property owner remains liable under California's peculiar risk doctrine even after hiring a contractor, because the owner cannot delegate away responsibility for work that poses inherent dangers to visitors, customers, and neighbors. You may have claims against all three simultaneously, each backed by separate insurance coverage.

Time limit
