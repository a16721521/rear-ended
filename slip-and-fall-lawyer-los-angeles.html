Premises liability

Property owners knew — or should have known.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of care on every property owner — commercial, residential, and government. When a hazardous condition exists and a property owner fails to repair it or warn visitors, they are liable for the injuries that result. The question is not whether you fell. It is whether the property owner met their duty.

The most common defense is "open and obvious" — the argument that a reasonable person would have seen and avoided the hazard. But this doctrine has significant limits under California law. Distractions, poor lighting, and the nature of the hazard itself can make an otherwise visible condition legally non-obvious. And even when a hazard is partially visible, property owners still have a duty to eliminate it if it creates an unreasonable risk.

Slip and fall cases produce some of the most serious injuries we see — hip fractures requiring replacement surgery, traumatic brain injuries from head contact with hard floors, and spinal injuries from violent backward falls. The severity often does not match the seeming simplicity of the incident, which is exactly why insurance adjusters work so hard to minimize these claims.

Law Dog handles premises liability on pure contingency. We advance all costs — investigation, experts, record compilation — and collect only from the recovery. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), you can recover even if you were partly at fault. If the hazard was on government property, Government Code § 911.2 gives you just six months. Contact us now.

Immediately after

What to do after a slip and fall.

The next few hours matter enormously. Property owners and their insurers move fast to document the scene in their favor — and to remove the hazard. Here is what to do before they get the chance.

Tell us what happened →
1
At the scene — immediate
Report the fall to the property manager

Notify whoever is in charge — store manager, building supervisor, hotel front desk — and ask them to create an incident report. Get a copy before you leave. This creates an official record the property owner cannot later deny. Never say you are "fine" or "okay." You don't know yet.

2
At the scene — immediate
Photograph the hazard before they fix it

Photograph the specific condition that caused your fall: the wet floor, the cracked pavement, the broken stair, the missing handrail. If there was no wet floor sign, photograph that too. Note the exact location, time, and any surveillance cameras. Property owners frequently clean up hazards within minutes of a fall.

3
At the scene — immediate
Get witness contact information

Anyone nearby who saw you fall, saw the hazard before you fell, or heard you report it to staff is a potential witness. Get names and phone numbers before they leave. Witnesses who aren't documented at the scene become very difficult to locate later.

4
Same day — critical
See a doctor. Don't wait.

Hip fractures, spinal injuries, and head injuries from falls are not always immediately apparent. Adrenaline masks pain. A same-day medical visit establishes causation and creates the record your claim depends on. A gap in treatment — even 48 hours — becomes an argument that your injuries weren't caused by the fall.

5
Same day
Preserve the clothing and shoes you were wearing

Your footwear is evidence. Insurers often argue that your shoes were inappropriate for the conditions. Preserve them — don't clean them, don't throw them out. Photograph them while still at the scene if possible, ideally while you're still wearing them.

6
As soon as possible
Call Law Dog before you talk to the property's insurer

The property owner's insurer will call quickly — often within 24 hours. They want a recorded statement before you have legal representation. Do not give one. Do not sign anything. Surveillance footage from the property is typically overwritten in 24–72 hours. The sooner we send preservation letters, the more evidence we can lock down.

What we document and fight for

Slip and fall injuries
are rarely minor.

Falls are a leading cause of traumatic injury. Insurers routinely minimize the severity of these claims. We retain medical experts who document every consequence — present and future.

Hip Fractures
The most severe and common fall injury in older adults. Often requires hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation. Risk of complications and secondary conditions is significant and well-documented.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Head contact with a hard floor during a backward or sideways fall can cause concussion, skull fracture, or hemorrhage. Cognitive changes, memory loss, and personality shifts may not appear for days or weeks after the fall.
Spinal Injuries
A backward fall compresses the spine on impact, causing herniated discs, vertebral fractures, or spinal cord damage with partial or permanent neurological effects. Chronic pain following spinal injuries can be permanent.
Broken Wrists
The natural instinct to break a fall with your hands leads to wrist fractures — often comminuted (shattered) fractures requiring surgery and hardware. Grip strength and fine motor function may be permanently reduced.
Knee Injuries
Impact or twisting during a fall can tear the ACL, MCL, or meniscus. These injuries frequently require surgery and extended physical therapy, with variable long-term outcomes that affect mobility and quality of life.
Shoulder Injuries
Rotator cuff tears from a fall or from bracing impact are common. Full tears often require surgical repair and months of rehabilitation — with incomplete functional recovery that limits work and daily activities.
Ankle Fractures
Tripping and falling on an uneven surface commonly causes ankle fractures, sometimes with ligament involvement. Surgery, casting, and limited mobility during recovery affect employment and daily function.
Back Injuries
Lumbar fractures, disc herniation, and muscle and ligament damage from fall impact. Chronic back pain after a slip and fall can be permanent and significantly affects quality of life, employment, and physical capacity.
Soft Tissue Injuries
Contusions, sprains, and muscle tears that insurers aggressively minimize. Ligament and tendon injuries cause lasting pain and instability that affects daily function — and they don't always show up on standard imaging.
Property owner playbook exposed

What every premises
liability adjuster is trained to say.

Property owners and their insurers have a standard script for slip and fall claims. It starts with the assumption that you were careless and works backward. We have heard every argument — and we know exactly how to counter each one.

Tactic 01
"You weren't watching where you were going."
Contributory inattention is a comparative fault argument — not a bar to recovery. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault, not eliminated. And the argument only works if the hazard was actually visible and avoidable. When a floor is wet without adequate signage, or a stairway is poorly lit, the property owner's negligence — not your attention — is the primary cause.
Tactic 02
"It was an open and obvious hazard."
The open and obvious doctrine has real limits under California law. Courts consider whether the visitor was distracted, whether lighting made the hazard non-obvious, and whether the owner should have anticipated people would encounter it without noticing. Even when a hazard is visible, owners still have a duty to eliminate it if it creates an unreasonable risk — the doctrine is a comparative fault argument, not a complete defense.
Tactic 03
"We had no notice of the condition."
Property owners are liable when they created the condition and when they had constructive notice — meaning they should have known through reasonable maintenance and inspection. A recurring leak, a long-standing crack, or a condition that employees walked past regularly creates constructive notice. We investigate maintenance records, inspection logs, and prior incident reports to prove what the owner knew and when.
Tactic 04
"Your footwear was inappropriate."
California law does not require visitors to wear specific footwear on commercial or residential property. Shoe choice may be raised as a comparative fault argument — but only for injuries directly attributable to footwear, and only to the extent your choice contributed to the fall. It does not reduce recovery for all injuries and is not a complete defense.
Tactic 05
"Your pre-existing condition made this worse."
The eggshell plaintiff rule is well-established California law: a defendant takes a plaintiff as they find them. If you had a prior hip condition, osteoporosis, or previous back injury that made you more susceptible to severe injury, that does not limit your recovery. The property owner is liable for the full extent of the injury, including aggravation of every pre-existing condition.
Tactic 06
"Here's a quick settlement — just sign here."
Early offers are designed to close the case before the full extent of your injuries is known — before you learn whether you need surgery, whether the TBI symptoms are permanent, how long recovery will take. Once you sign a release, the case is permanently closed. Never accept a first offer without independent medical and legal evaluation.

Filing deadlines

Two years. Six months for government property. The clock is running.

Two years from the date of the fall under CCP § 335.1 for claims against a private property owner — a store, restaurant, landlord, or commercial building. Six months under Government Code § 911.2 for any fall on government-maintained property — a city sidewalk, public park, county building, school, or transit facility. Miss either deadline and the claim is permanently extinguished regardless of how serious the injuries were. Surveillance footage is also overwritten in 24–72 hours. Questions people ask us about slip and fall claims.

Premises liability holds property owners responsible for injuries caused by unsafe conditions on their property. Under Civil Code § 1714, every person owes a duty of ordinary care in the management of their property. When a property owner knows or should know about a dangerous condition and fails to repair it or warn visitors, they are liable for the injuries that result. This applies to commercial owners, residential landlords, retail stores, restaurants, and government agencies.
You must establish four elements: (1) the property owner owed you a duty of care; (2) the owner created the hazardous condition or knew — or should have known — about it; (3) the owner failed to repair the condition or warn you about it; and (4) that failure caused your injuries. "Should have known" is key — a recurring leak, a long-standing crack, or a condition that employees walked past repeatedly creates constructive notice.
