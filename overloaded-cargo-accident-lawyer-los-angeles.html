Overloaded & improperly secured cargo crashes

When cargo shifts or falls, federal securement rules tell the story.

A fully loaded semi already weighs up to 80,000 pounds — the federal gross vehicle weight limit on interstate highways. When a truck exceeds that limit, or when cargo inside the trailer is improperly packed and secured, two distinct crash scenarios unfold. In a load shift, freight migrating inside the trailer raises the center of gravity or throws lateral weight to one side, destabilizing the truck mid-corner or under sudden braking until it sways uncontrollably or rolls. In a cargo spill, unsecured or inadequately tied-down items escape from flatbeds or open trailers directly into the path of following traffic — creating a sudden obstacle field that triggers multi-vehicle pileups with no warning time.

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration regulations under 49 CFR Part 393 Subpart I set mandatory cargo securement standards: specific tie-down working load limits, required securement devices by cargo category, and mandatory driver re-inspection of the load after the first 50 miles and at every subsequent stop. On California roads, Caltrans also requires special oversize/overweight permits for loads exceeding standard dimensions or the 80,000-pound federal limit — permits that can only be legally obtained if the carrier has verified the load falls within parameters the permit covers. Overloaded trucks increase stopping distance, overstress brake systems and tires, and produce the exact mechanical conditions that prevent a driver from responding to an emergency in time.

What makes overloaded cargo cases distinct is the number of parties who can share liability. The motor carrier has an independent duty under FMCSA rules to inspect cargo before accepting it and at required intervals during transport. The shipper — the company that packed and tendered the load — is liable when it misrepresented the load's weight, packed it in a way that violated securement standards, or understated cargo dimensions to avoid permit requirements. The freight broker who arranged the haul can bear responsibility if it placed the cargo with a carrier it knew or should have known lacked the equipment or authority to transport it safely. Third-party loading companies are directly liable when their crews physically secured the cargo incorrectly.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after an overloaded cargo crash.

Carriers, shippers, and their insurers activate immediately after a cargo crash. The shipping manifests, weight tickets, and securement logs that prove negligence can be altered or lost if you don't act fast.

1
At the scene
Call 911 and request a full commercial vehicle investigation.

Crashes involving commercial trucks — especially those with cargo on the roadway — typically trigger involvement by the California Highway Patrol and may require a commercial vehicle inspector. Get the officer's name, badge number, and report number. CHP officers are trained to document cargo conditions, securement device failures, and trailer configuration, all of which become part of the official record.

2
At the scene
Photograph everything — the truck, the load, the roadway, all vehicles.

Document the position and condition of any cargo on the roadway, the securement straps, chains, or tarps still on the trailer, any debris field, the trailer's axle configuration, and markings identifying the carrier. If the truck rolled, photograph the load distribution inside the trailer if visible. Capture weight rating placards on the trailer and any permit placards on the cab. These photographs may be the only record of how the load was actually secured before the carrier moves or removes the truck.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney to send a spoliation letter before manifests disappear.

Shipping manifests, bills of lading, weight station tickets, cargo inspection logs, and loading company records are the core evidence in overloaded cargo cases. Carriers and shippers have been known to misplace or destroy documentation once litigation is anticipated. A spoliation letter sent immediately by your attorney places the carrier, shipper, loader, and freight broker on formal legal notice to preserve all records — and sanctions are available if they don't.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment and follow through completely.

Cargo crashes — whether a rollover, a debris strike, or a multi-vehicle pileup — involve high-force, multi-directional impacts. Spinal injuries, internal bleeding, and traumatic brain injuries can present with delayed symptoms. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this crash and prevents the defense from arguing your injuries predated the accident or resulted from something else.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the carrier's insurer.

The trucking company's insurer and the shipper's insurer may both contact you quickly. Their goal is to get statements on record that minimize their exposure — suggesting the cargo was properly secured, that you were following too closely to stop in time, or that the debris was unavoidable. Let your attorney handle all communication with all carriers, shippers, and their insurers. Statements made before the evidence is fully developed can be used against you.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document every day of lost income.

Overloaded cargo crash injuries — particularly those from rollovers and high-speed debris strikes — often require prolonged treatment: surgeries, rehabilitation, specialist follow-ups, and adaptive equipment over months or years. Every medical visit, prescription, therapy session, and day of missed work builds the damages case. Document everything and share it with your attorney regularly so the damages picture stays current.

What overloaded cargo crashes cause

A rollover or a debris strike at
highway speed is catastrophic.

Cargo shift rollovers crush smaller vehicles under the trailer. Fallen freight at highway speed becomes a projectile. Both mechanisms produce severe, life-altering injuries.

Traumatic Brain Injury
Multi-vehicle pileups triggered by fallen cargo produce chain-reaction impacts where vehicles strike one another at closing speeds. Occupants sustain repeated head impacts — against windows, steering wheels, airbags — producing concussion, diffuse axonal injury, and more severe TBI that may not be immediately apparent at the scene.
Spinal Cord Injuries
A rollover caused by shifting cargo collapses the passenger compartment from multiple axes simultaneously. Vertical compression, lateral bending, and rotational forces applied to the spine in rapid sequence produce vertebral fractures, disc herniation at multiple levels, and spinal cord injuries ranging from partial sensory deficits to complete paralysis.
Penetrating Trauma
Industrial cargo — steel coils, lumber, pipe, machinery — that escapes a trailer at highway speed becomes a high-velocity projectile. Penetrating injuries to the torso, extremities, and head require immediate surgical intervention and may cause damage to major blood vessels, organs, and neural structures that is irreversible.
Crush Injuries
When an overloaded semi rolls onto a smaller vehicle, the weight differential produces catastrophic compartment intrusion. Crush syndrome — muscle breakdown from prolonged compression — can cause kidney failure and cardiac arrhythmia even after the victim is extricated. Long-term outcomes include limb amputation and the need for dialysis.
Internal Organ Damage
Blunt abdominal trauma from vehicle deformation or direct cargo impact can rupture the liver, spleen, or kidneys and cause mesenteric vascular tears with internal hemorrhaging. These injuries are frequently not apparent until hemorrhagic shock develops, making emergency imaging and surgical evaluation critical in any cargo crash where the abdomen was exposed to force.
Burns
Rollover crashes carry elevated fire risk: ruptured diesel fuel tanks, friction-ignited fires from the trailer dragging on pavement, and — when the cargo is chemical, petroleum, or hazardous materials — toxic vapor exposure or explosion. Overloaded trucks also stress fuel system components, increasing the probability of post-crash fuel leaks and ignition.
What they don't want you to know

The carrier's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Carriers, shippers, and their combined defense teams have strategies for deflecting liability. Here's what they typically argue in overloaded cargo cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The truck was within the legal weight limit — we were fully compliant"
Being at or under 80,000 pounds doesn't mean the cargo was loaded safely. Even within overall weight limits, improper weight distribution — heavy items loaded high, cargo stacked asymmetrically, or weight concentrated over the wrong axles — raises the center of gravity and creates rollover instability that the gross weight number doesn't capture. Axle weight distribution records, and the loading company's own documentation, often reveal exactly where compliance failed.
Tactic 02
"The shipper packed it — that's not our responsibility"
Carriers try to isolate liability on the shipper or loading company when cargo securement is defective. But FMCSA regulations place an independent obligation on the motor carrier to inspect cargo securement before accepting a load and at each mandatory stop. A carrier that accepted a load without verifying securement compliance — and failed to perform required 50-mile and 150-mile re-inspections — has its own direct liability regardless of what the shipper did.
Tactic 03
"The securement was inspected and passed before departure"
Pre-departure inspection records are often rubber-stamped rather than genuinely performed. FMCSA requires re-inspection at the first 50 miles and at each stop — and many carriers' en-route inspection logs are blank or fabricated. When the inspection logs show check marks at intervals that don't match the driver's ELD movement data, or when cargo shifted despite claimed compliance, the pre-trip inspection defense collapses under cross-examination of the actual records.
Tactic 04
"You had enough distance to avoid the debris — this was driver error"
When cargo falls from a truck, the carrier's insurer often argues that a following vehicle had adequate reaction time and stopping distance. At highway speeds, reaction time and stopping distance combine to make avoiding sudden large debris on the roadway physically impossible in many scenarios — and the carrier created that hazard. Accident reconstruction establishes the timeline from cargo fall to impact and confirms that the obstacle appeared with no practical avoidance window.

Questions & answers

Overloaded cargo truck accident FAQ.

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration regulations under 49 CFR Part 393 Subpart I establish detailed cargo securement standards — specific tie-down requirements based on cargo type, minimum working load limits for securement devices, and mandatory re-inspection of cargo after the first 50 miles and at each change of duty status. When carriers skip these inspections or loaders pack freight incorrectly, two types of crashes result. In a load shift, cargo moving inside the trailer raises the center of gravity or shifts weight laterally, causing the truck to sway, lose stability, or roll over — particularly during lane changes and curves. In a cargo spill, unsecured or inadequately secured items fall from flatbeds or open trailers onto the roadway, creating obstacles that trigger multi-vehicle pileups. Overloaded trucks compound both hazards: exceeding the 80,000-pound federal gross vehicle weight limit increases stopping distance, degrades brake performance, and accelerates tire wear to the point of failure.
Multiple parties typically share liability in overloaded cargo crash cases. The motor carrier bears responsibility for accepting a load without confirming it complied with FMCSA securement standards and for failing to conduct required en-route cargo checks. The shipper — the company that packed and tendered the load — is liable when it packed cargo incorrectly or misrepresented the load's weight or contents. The freight broker who arranged the shipment can face liability if it matched cargo to a carrier it knew or should have known was non-compliant. Third-party loading companies are directly liable when they performed the physical loading and secured it improperly. Under California Civil Code § 1431.2's pure comparative fault rule, each defendant is responsible for their proportionate share of fault — and every named defendant puts pressure on the others to settle.
Shipping manifests and bills of lading identify what the load was, its declared weight, and who packed it. Weight tickets from weigh stations show whether the truck was actually at or over the legal limit when it entered California. Cargo securement records — required pre-trip and en-route inspection logs — reveal whether the driver performed the mandatory checks FMCSA requires at the first 50 miles and at each stop. Photos or video from the crash scene showing cargo on the roadway, inside a rolled trailer, or the securement devices themselves are powerful physical evidence. The driver's ELD data shows whether re-inspection stops occurred as required. Photographs of the load at origin, if taken by the shipper or loader, can show exactly how cargo was packed before departure. Black box data provides speed and braking profiles at the moment of impact.
Title 49 CFR Part 393 Subpart I sets out the federal cargo securement standards that apply to all commercial motor vehicles. Key requirements include: cargo must be contained, immobilized, or secured against shifting or falling during transport; tie-downs must meet specific working load limit requirements based on cargo weight; drivers must inspect the load and all securing devices before departure; cargo must be re-inspected after the first 50 miles of a trip, after each change of driver, and at every three-hour or 150-mile interval. Separate standards apply to different cargo categories — such as logs, metal coils, vehicles, and concrete pipe — with more stringent requirements for high-mass items. Violations of these standards are federal safety violations that constitute direct evidence of negligence in a civil lawsuit.
