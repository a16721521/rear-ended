Truck underride crashes

The car goes under the trailer. Everything above the dashboard is gone.

A truck underride accident happens when a passenger vehicle slides beneath the trailer of a large truck — either from the rear or the side. The trailer bed, riding roughly at windshield height, shears through the car's roof, A-pillars, and upper cabin. Every crash protection system the car was designed with — crumple zones, airbags, seatbelt pretensioners — is bypassed entirely. The result is among the most catastrophic collision outcomes on American roadways.

Federal law addresses this problem incompletely. 49 CFR § 393.86 requires rear underride guards on trailers — but the standard sets minimum strength thresholds that many guards fail to meet in actual crash conditions. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety has repeatedly demonstrated that guards meeting the federal minimum buckle or detach under real-world impact forces. Side underride guards, which would prevent lateral crashes, are not federally required at all — despite years of proposed legislation and clear evidence that they save lives.

When a guard fails, multiple parties bear liability: the guard manufacturer for a defective product, the trailer manufacturer for installing an inadequate system, and the carrier for operating equipment that doesn't protect other road users. California product liability law imposes strict liability on defective products — meaning you don't need to prove negligence, only that the guard was defective and caused your injuries. When the crash involves a poorly lit or unmarked trailer, 49 CFR § 393.11 reflective marking and lighting violations add an independent basis for negligence.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), even a partial fault finding against you only reduces your recovery proportionally — it does not eliminate your claim. What to do after an underride crash.

At the scene
Call 911 and request a full accident investigation.

Underride crashes typically involve fatalities or catastrophic injuries — request the full accident investigation team, not just a patrol report. Get the responding officer's name, badge number, and report number. Note whether the trailer was stopped, moving, or disabled when the collision occurred.

2
At the scene
Photograph the trailer, the guard, and the point of penetration.

Document whether the underride guard is still attached, bent, detached, or absent. Photograph the trailer's reflective tape condition, rear lighting, and any markings. Capture the car's position relative to the trailer — how far under it went tells the story of whether the guard did its job.

3
Immediately
Demand preservation of the trailer and the car.

The trailer — including the underride guard — and the car must both be impounded and preserved for engineering analysis. Carriers and salvage yards will repair, scrap, or destroy vehicles quickly. We send spoliation preservation letters to the carrier, the insurer, and the salvage yard within hours of engagement to prevent this.

4
Same day
Get to an emergency room — document everything.

Underride crash injuries are typically severe: head trauma, spinal cord damage, crush injuries, amputations. Full imaging — CT, MRI — establishes the baseline for every injury caused by the crash. If the victim cannot act for themselves, a family member should ensure records are created and preserved.

Within 48 hours
Identify the carrier, the trailer manufacturer, and the guard manufacturer.

Underride cases frequently involve multiple defendants: the motor carrier operating the truck, the trailer manufacturer, and the company that manufactured the underride guard. The USDOT number on the truck, the trailer VIN, and the guard's manufacturer markings all establish the chain. We pull FMCSA records to identify the carrier's safety history and prior violations.

Call Law Dog before the carrier's insurer contacts you.

Trucking insurers deploy rapid response teams to crash scenes — sometimes within hours — to control the narrative and preserve evidence in their favor. Do not give a recorded statement. What underride impacts do

The car's safety systems
are bypassed entirely.

The trailer edge strikes at windshield height. Crumple zones, airbags, and seatbelt systems cannot protect occupants from a force that enters the cabin from above.

Decapitation / Fatal Head Injuries
The trailer edge shears through the windshield and roof at head height. This is the signature injury pattern of underride crashes and the primary reason these collisions have among the highest fatality rates of any crash type.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Roof intrusion into the passenger compartment causes direct cranial impact with the trailer undercarriage, structural beams, and collapsing roof components. Even survivors face severe closed-head injuries requiring lifelong care.
Spinal Cord Injuries
Catastrophic neck and cervical compression occurs as the roof collapses downward. The vertical crushing force is unlike a standard frontal collision — the spine is loaded from above, a direction the car's structure was never designed to resist.
Chest Crush Injuries
The dashboard, steering column, and collapsing roof compress the occupant's torso. Rib fractures, flail chest, cardiac contusion, and aortic tears are common in the confined space created by a partially crushed cabin.
Facial / Skull Fractures
Direct impact between the occupant's face and the trailer undercarriage — axles, cross-members, and cargo floor — causes severe maxillofacial trauma, orbital fractures, and jaw injuries requiring extensive surgical reconstruction.
Amputations
Extremities caught in the deforming vehicle structure or pinned between the car and trailer components are severed or crushed beyond salvage. Upper extremity amputations are disproportionately common in underride crashes due to the direction of force.
What they don't want you to know

They'll defend a guard that failed to do its job.

Carriers and manufacturers have well-rehearsed defenses for underride crashes. Here's what to expect and why each one fails.

Tactic 01
"Our underride guard met federal standards"
Federal standards under 49 CFR § 393.86 are minimum baselines that don't account for real-world crash forces. IIHS testing has proven that many guards meeting the federal minimum buckle or detach on impact. Meeting the minimum standard does not mean the guard was adequate — it means the standard is inadequate.
Tactic 02
"The car driver wasn't paying attention"
Trailers that are poorly lit, unmarked, or stopped on the roadway without proper warning devices create the hazard regardless of driver attention. Under 49 CFR § 393.11, carriers must maintain reflective markings and rear lighting. A trailer that's invisible at night is a hazard the carrier created, not one the driver failed to avoid.
Tactic 03
"The guard was intact after the crash"
A guard can remain bolted to the trailer and still fail completely. The question isn't whether the guard stayed attached — it's whether the guard stopped the car from going under the trailer. If the car penetrated beneath the trailer bed, the guard failed its purpose regardless of its post-crash condition.
Tactic 04
"This was a side underride — no guard is required"
The absence of a federal side-guard mandate does not eliminate the carrier's duty of reasonable care under California law. Side guards are commercially available, proven effective, and increasingly adopted by safety-conscious fleets. The fact that a regulation hasn't caught up to the technology doesn't excuse the carrier from using it.
