Structural failure premises liability

When a balcony, ceiling, or staircase gives way, deferred maintenance is usually why.

Structural failure claims cover a building component that collapses under normal use — balcony and deck collapses, ceiling cave-ins, staircase and stairwell failures, scaffolding and construction platform failures, and building material failures like corroded railings or rotted support beams. These collapses are rarely sudden accidents. Under Civil Code § 1714, every property owner and operator owes a duty of reasonable care to keep these structures safe, and the physical evidence in a collapse — water intrusion, dry rot, corrosion, or original construction that never met code — almost always shows the failure was years in the making.

California responded to this exact pattern after the 2015 Berkeley balcony collapse that killed six students when a rotted, water-damaged balcony gave way under normal weight. SB 721 (Health & Safety Code § 17973) now requires owners of apartment buildings with three or more units to have a licensed professional inspect "exterior elevated elements" — balconies, decks, stairways, and walkways — at least once every six years. SB 326 (Civil Code § 5551) imposes a parallel requirement on condominium and homeowners association boards. A skipped inspection, or an inspection report that flagged deterioration the owner ignored, is often the clearest proof of negligence in a structural failure case.

Landlords carry an additional, independent duty: Civil Code § 1941 requires rental units and their structural components to be kept in habitable condition, which means a collapsed balcony or ceiling inside your own unit is both a habitability violation and a premises liability claim. And liability rarely stops with one defendant — the property owner, the property management company, the original contractor or builder, and the structural engineer who designed or last inspected the element can all bear responsibility.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after a structural collapse.

Property owners often repair or demolish a collapsed structure quickly — for safety and to limit liability. The permit history and inspection records that prove negligence can become harder to obtain if you don't act fast.

1
At the scene
Call 911 and request emergency responders.

A structural collapse can leave the surrounding area unstable — call 911 immediately so paramedics can treat the injured and fire department personnel can assess whether the remaining structure is safe. If the building department red-tags the property, that report becomes early evidence of how serious the defect was.

2
At the scene
Photograph the collapsed structure and visible defects.

Document the collapsed material, exposed rot, rust, cracked supports, and the surrounding area. Property owners often move quickly to repair or demolish a collapsed balcony, staircase, or ceiling — sometimes within days — which can destroy the physical evidence of what actually caused the failure.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved before repairs or demolition begin.

Property owners and their insurers have a strong incentive to repair or remove a collapsed structure quickly — both for safety and to limit liability exposure. An attorney can send an immediate preservation letter demanding the owner retain the failed materials and preserve the scene, and can pull building permits, inspection records, and SB 721/SB 326 compliance history before they become harder to obtain.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment and follow through.

Structural collapses often involve falls from height or being struck by falling material. Internal bleeding, spinal compression, and traumatic brain injury can present with delayed symptoms even after a fall of a single story. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this collapse.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the owner's insurer.

The property owner's insurer will often contact you quickly, sometimes suggesting the collapse was sudden and unforeseeable or that you were somewhere you shouldn't have been. Let your attorney handle all communication with the owner, property manager, and their insurer.

6
Ongoing
Keep medical records and request the building's permit history.

Structural failure injuries frequently require extended treatment, and the building department's permit and inspection files are public record that can independently confirm whether the property was properly built and maintained. Keep every medical record and share the permit history with your attorney as you obtain it.

What structural collapses cause

A structure that fails
under normal use is catastrophic.

Structural failures often mean a fall from height or being struck by falling building material with no warning. The injuries reflect that force.

Traumatic Brain Injury
Falls from a collapsing balcony or staircase, or being struck by falling ceiling material, transmit significant force to the head. Concussion, skull fracture, and more severe TBI can result even from a fall of a single story.
Spinal Cord Injuries
A fall from an elevated structure like a balcony or deck compresses the spine on impact. Vertebral fractures and spinal cord damage can result in partial or complete paralysis requiring lifelong care and adaptive equipment.
Crush Injuries
Ceiling and scaffolding collapses can trap victims under heavy building material — drywall, joists, concrete, and metal framing. Crush injuries can cause compartment syndrome and require emergency extrication and surgery.
Multiple Fractures
Falls from a collapsed structure commonly cause fractures to the wrist, ankle, hip, and spine as the body absorbs the impact, often requiring surgical repair with plates, screws, or rods and extended rehabilitation.
Lacerations & Puncture Wounds
Splintered wood, exposed rebar, and sharp metal edges in collapsed structural material frequently cause deep lacerations and puncture wounds requiring surgical repair and carrying significant infection risk.
Wrongful Death
The 2015 Berkeley balcony collapse killed six people in a single incident. Structural failures involving falls from height or falling material carry a high risk of fatal injury, and families can pursue a wrongful death claim against every liable party.
What they don't want you to know

The property owner's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Property owners and their insurers carry defense strategies built for structural collapse claims. Here's what they typically argue — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The collapse was sudden and unforeseeable"
Property owners routinely claim a structural failure came without warning. But collapses caused by dry rot, corrosion, or water intrusion develop over years, not seconds — SB 721 and SB 326 inspection reports, prior tenant complaints, and maintenance records often show the defect was documented and ignored long before it failed.
Tactic 02
"The building met code when it was constructed"
An owner's obligation doesn't end at the certificate of occupancy. Owners have an ongoing duty under Civil Code § 1714 to maintain structural elements in safe condition regardless of the building's age, and a structure that met code decades ago can become dangerous through deferred maintenance the owner failed to address.
Tactic 03
"That's the HOA's, tenant's, or contractor's responsibility — not ours"
Structural failure cases frequently involve overlapping duties. An owner, a property manager, an HOA board, and the original contractor can all bear responsibility — we identify every party with control over the structure and every party whose work or inspection failed, so liability isn't shifted away from a defendant with insurance coverage.
Tactic 04
"You were warned not to use that area"
A posted sign or verbal warning doesn't excuse an owner from the underlying duty to repair a known hazard. Under Civil Code § 1714, an owner who knows a structural element is dangerous must fix it or take it out of service — not simply warn people and continue to allow access.

Questions & answers

Structural failure claim FAQ.

Structural failure claims cover injuries caused by a building component that gives way due to inadequate construction, deferred maintenance, or a hidden defect — balcony and deck collapses, ceiling cave-ins, staircase and stairwell failures, scaffolding and construction platform failures, and building material failures such as corroded railings or rotted support beams. Under Civil Code § 1714, property owners and operators owe a duty of reasonable care to keep these elements safe, and a collapse is rarely a random accident — it is almost always traceable to water intrusion, dry rot, corrosion, or original construction that didn't meet code.
SB 721 (codified at Health & Safety Code § 17973) requires owners of apartment buildings with three or more units to have a licensed professional inspect "exterior elevated elements" — balconies, decks, stairways, and walkways supported by wood framing — at least once every six years. SB 326 (Civil Code § 5551) imposes a similar inspection requirement on condominium and homeowners association boards for the same elevated elements. Both laws were enacted after the 2015 Berkeley balcony collapse that killed six students when a rotted, water-damaged balcony gave way. If the property owner or HOA skipped a required inspection, or ignored a report flagging deterioration, that failure is powerful evidence of negligence in your case.
Liability often extends to multiple parties. The property owner or landlord can be liable for deferred maintenance and failing to conduct required inspections. The property management company can be liable if it was responsible for upkeep and ignored warning signs like sagging, cracking, or visible rot. The original contractor or builder can be liable if the structure was built with inadequate materials or in violation of the California Building Code. The structural engineer who designed or later inspected the element can be liable for a defective design or a negligent inspection that missed the defect. We investigate ownership, management contracts, permit history, and inspection records to identify every responsible party.
Building permit history and Certificate of Occupancy records show whether the structure was built and modified to code. Prior inspection reports — including any SB 721/SB 326 inspections — show whether known defects were disclosed and ignored. Maintenance and repair logs reveal whether the owner deferred needed work to save money. Prior tenant or visitor complaints about sagging floors, cracking, or water stains establish that the owner had notice. And a structural engineer's post-collapse analysis identifies the root cause — water intrusion, dry rot, corrosion, or a construction defect — and ties it directly to the owner's or contractor's negligence.

But the evidence that wins structural failure cases — building permit history, SB 721/SB 326 inspection reports, maintenance logs, and prior complaint records — gets harder to obtain the longer a property sits repaired or rebuilt. If a government entity owns or operates the building, the deadline is six months under Government Code § 911.2. The sooner we start, the more evidence we lock down.
