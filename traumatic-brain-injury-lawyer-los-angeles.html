Traumatic brain injury

Normal imaging doesn't mean no injury. It means they're not looking hard enough.

Standard CT scans and MRIs detect structural damage — bleeds, fractures, large contusions. They are not sensitive to the diffuse axonal injury, microhemorrhages, and white matter disruption that characterize mild and moderate TBI. A patient can have a perfectly normal MRI and still suffer from significant cognitive impairment, personality changes, chronic headaches, light and sound sensitivity, memory deficits, and inability to return to work. This is the central challenge in TBI litigation — and the reason insurers dismiss mild TBI claims.

TBI ranges from mild (what used to be called concussion) to severe (coma, vegetative state). But "mild" is a medical classification of initial presentation, not of long-term impact. Post-concussion syndrome, which can persist for years, is a well-documented clinical entity. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) research has shifted public understanding of what repeated or undertreated brain injury actually does. A single significant concussion from a car accident can alter a person's life permanently.

Neuropsychological testing — a battery of standardized cognitive assessments administered by a licensed neuropsychologist — documents the specific deficits that imaging misses: processing speed, working memory, executive function, attention, and behavioral regulation. This testing, combined with treating neurologist records, functional MRI where appropriate, and before-and-after testimony from family and employers, builds a TBI case that stands up to defense scrutiny.

TBI damages go beyond medical bills. Lost earning capacity (the difference between what the client earned before and their diminished post-injury capacity), cost of neuropsychological treatment, cognitive rehabilitation, psychiatric care, medication, and the non-economic value of personality change, relationship disruption, and loss of enjoyment of life. Step-by-step

What to do after a head injury.

The actions you take in the first week after a TBI have an outsized effect on your medical outcome and your legal case. Follow these steps in order.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Tell first responders about any head impact.

Tell first responders about any head impact, loss of consciousness, or confusion — even brief. "I blacked out for a second" or "I'm confused" should be documented in the emergency report. TBI triage begins at the scene, and early documentation of altered consciousness is critical evidence.

2
At the emergency room
Request a neurological evaluation, not just a trauma survey.

Emergency departments are focused on ruling out life-threatening bleeds. Ask specifically about concussion evaluation, symptom documentation, and follow-up neurology referral. Get a copy of every test ordered and the results.

3
Within 72 hours
Track every symptom in writing.

Start a daily journal: headache severity (1–10), cognitive fog, light/sound sensitivity, sleep quality, emotional state, any moments of confusion or memory gaps. This contemporaneous record is powerful evidence — and it catches the trajectory of symptoms that clinicians may not capture in brief follow-up visits.

4
Within one week
See a neurologist or TBI specialist.

Emergency room clearance is not a TBI diagnosis. A neurologist can order functional MRI (fMRI), SPECT imaging, or neuropsychological testing that reveals what standard CT and MRI miss. The sooner specialized testing occurs, the stronger the causal link to the crash.

5
Before returning to work
Get medical clearance in writing — and do not rush it.

Return to work before full cognitive recovery can worsen TBI outcomes and creates the false impression that you've recovered — which insurers exploit. Your physician's documented return-to-work assessment is evidence of functional impairment.

6
Before any settlement discussions
Insist on complete neuropsychological testing first.

A full neuropsychological battery takes 6–8 hours and produces a comprehensive report documenting every cognitive domain affected. No TBI case should be valued without this foundation. We will not recommend settlement until this testing is complete and reviewed by our team.

Types of TBI

What the impact
actually does to the brain.

TBI encompasses a spectrum of injuries — from invisible cognitive disruption to life-altering structural damage. We handle every point on that spectrum.

Mild TBI / Concussion & Post-Concussion Syndrome
The most commonly litigated TBI. Initial presentation may appear minor, but post-concussion syndrome — persistent headache, cognitive impairment, sleep disruption, depression, and light/sound sensitivity — can last months or years. Insurers aggressively deny mild TBI claims because imaging is normal. Neuropsychological testing is the primary tool to establish and document these injuries.
Diffuse Axonal Injury (DAI)
Caused by rotational acceleration-deceleration forces that stretch and tear axons throughout the white matter. DAI does not appear on standard MRI but is visible on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Ranges from mild cognitive impairment to coma. DAI from high-speed crashes is often the mechanism behind the worst long-term outcomes in patients with initially "normal" scans.
Subdural Hematoma
Bleeding between the brain surface and the dura, often from tearing of bridging veins. Can be acute (presenting immediately) or chronic (developing gradually over days to weeks). Older adults are particularly vulnerable. Surgical evacuation is frequently required. Residual cognitive and motor deficits are common following even successfully treated subdural hematomas.
Contusion & Intracerebral Hemorrhage
Bruising of brain tissue and bleeding within the brain parenchyma. Frontal and temporal lobe contusions from dashboard or steering wheel impacts cause personality changes, impulsivity, emotional regulation problems, and memory disruption — often more disabling in daily life than physical impairments, and frequently dismissed by insurers as "pre-existing psychiatric issues."
Cognitive & Executive Function Deficits
Even moderate TBI without obvious physical sequelae can produce devastating cognitive impairment: inability to multitask, difficulty with complex problem-solving, reduced processing speed, memory encoding failures, and executive dysfunction. A professional who earned $200,000 annually and can no longer perform complex cognitive work has suffered a catastrophic economic loss — even if they look "fine" externally.
Psychological Sequelae
TBI dramatically increases the risk of depression, anxiety, PTSD, and — in repeated or severe injuries — personality disorders. The neurobiological basis for post-TBI psychiatric symptoms is well-established. These psychological consequences are not character weakness — they are direct injury effects, fully compensable under California law, and often the primary driver of quality-of-life damage in mild TBI cases.
What they don't want you to know

The playbook they run on TBI victims.

Insurers follow a coordinated script to dismiss traumatic brain injury claims. Recognizing the tactics before they're deployed is the first line of defense.

Tactic 01
"The Imaging Is Normal" Defense
The most common dismissal of mild TBI claims. Insurers and their medical experts assert that a normal CT or MRI means no brain injury occurred. This assertion is scientifically outdated and consistently rejected by experienced neurologists. We retain neuropsychologists who administer comprehensive cognitive testing and, where appropriate, neurologists who interpret advanced imaging (DTI, fMRI, SPECT) that standard scans miss.
Tactic 02
The Pre-Existing Psychiatric Defense
Any documented history of depression, anxiety, ADHD, or prior head injury becomes a target. Insurers argue that current cognitive and psychological symptoms are pre-existing rather than crash-caused. We document the client's pre-crash baseline through employment records, academic records, tax returns (for earning capacity), and before-and-after testimony from family, friends, and employers who observed the cognitive change directly.
Tactic 03
Minimizing Neuropsychological Testing
Defense teams attack neuropsychological test results by arguing poor effort, symptom magnification, or invalid performance. We retain neuropsychologists who use validated performance validity tests (PVTs) as part of their battery — tests that measure effort independent of ability — so that genuine impairment is distinguished from simulation with scientific precision.
Tactic 04
Rapid Settlement Before Symptom Plateau
TBI symptoms evolve. The trajectory at 3 months is not the trajectory at 18 months. Insurers make early offers specifically to capture favorable moments before the full picture is clear. We track the symptom trajectory through serial neuropsychological testing, treating physician records, and functional observation before recommending any settlement figure.

Time limit

Two years by law. Neuropsychological documentation takes time — start now.

California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years from the date of injury to file. But TBI cases require early action for a different reason: neuropsychological testing documents the injury trajectory from close to the event. Testing administered 6 months post-crash tells a different — and often more powerful — story than testing done 18 months later, after partial recovery has occurred. If a government vehicle or road condition contributed, the deadline is six months under Government Code § 911.2. We move immediately.
