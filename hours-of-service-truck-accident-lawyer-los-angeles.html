Hours-of-service violation crashes

When a carrier's delivery schedule is more important than the law, the ELD records tell the story.

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration regulations under 49 CFR Part 395 impose strict driving limits on commercial truck drivers: no more than 11 hours of driving within a 14-hour on-duty window, a mandatory 10 consecutive hours off duty before beginning that window, and a required 30-minute rest break after 8 hours of continuous driving. Weekly driving is capped at 60 hours over 7 days or 70 hours over 8 days. These rules are not suggestions. They exist because federal crash research shows that fatigue impairs a driver's reaction time and hazard recognition as severely as alcohol, and that crash risk more than doubles after the 8th consecutive hour behind the wheel.

The electronic logging device (ELD) mandate under 49 CFR Part 395 Subpart B requires virtually all commercial trucks to use a tamper-resistant device that automatically records engine hours, vehicle movement, miles, and location. Unlike the paper logbooks that drivers could fill out however they chose — called "comic books" in the industry for how freely they were altered — ELDs create a continuous, objective record of actual driving time and rest periods. When an hours-of-service violation causes a crash, that ELD data is the most important piece of evidence in the case. It shows when the driver last rested, how many consecutive hours he or she had been driving, and whether the carrier's own dispatch system scheduled the driver in knowing violation of federal limits.

Carrier liability is direct and substantial. A carrier that schedules delivery windows that cannot be achieved within legal driving hours has created the violation — the driver is executing the carrier's instructions. When internal communications, dispatch records, and ELD data show that a pattern of HOS violations was known to management and tolerated, that conduct supports claims for punitive damages under California Civil Code § 3294 for conscious disregard of public safety. Log falsification by drivers or carriers compounds that exposure dramatically.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. What to do after an hours-of-service violation crash.

1
At the scene
Call 911 and request a full commercial vehicle accident investigation.

Crashes involving commercial trucks typically warrant a more thorough police investigation than standard passenger-vehicle collisions. CHP and some local agencies have specialized commercial vehicle inspection units. Get the responding officer's name, badge number, and report number. Note whether officers conducted any roadside inspection of the truck, and ask whether a post-crash drug and alcohol test was administered — federal regulations require it in crashes involving fatalities or injuries requiring hospitalization.

2
At the scene
Photograph the scene and note every detail about the truck and driver.

Document the truck's USDOT number and license plate — these identify the carrier and allow retrieval of its FMCSA safety record. Photograph the cab, any visible signage showing the carrier or fleet operator, and the condition of tires and exterior equipment. If witnesses observed the truck before the crash — drifting, varying speed, delayed braking — get their names and contact information. Note the time of day and any conditions suggesting fatigue: empty energy drink cans visible through the cab window, rumble strip marks, or evidence of lane drift before impact.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved to send a spoliation letter before the ELD data is overwritten.

ELD data retention periods vary by carrier and device — some systems overwrite data in as few as 30 days. Dispatch records, trip logs, and internal scheduling communications that show the carrier knew the driver would exceed HOS limits are also at risk. Trucking companies dispatch rapid-response teams to crash sites, and those teams work to document the scene in ways favorable to the carrier. Having your attorney send a formal legal preservation demand that day locks in the driver's full hours history, dispatch records, and communications between the carrier and driver in the days before the crash.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment and follow through completely.

High-speed impacts from fatigued truck drivers — who may fail to brake at all before impact — produce severe forces on occupant bodies. Internal injuries, spinal compression, and traumatic brain injuries frequently present with delayed symptoms. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this specific crash. Every subsequent visit, prescription, imaging study, and therapy session reinforces that record.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the carrier's insurer.

The trucking company's insurer will contact you promptly, often framing it as a formality. Their goal is a statement on record that can be used to limit their exposure — suggesting you were in the truck's blind spot, made a sudden lane change, or could have avoided the collision. In an hours-of-service case, they may also attempt to establish that the driver appeared alert and functional, undermining your fatigue theory before you have obtained the ELD data that proves otherwise. Let your attorney handle all communication with the carrier and its insurer.

6
Ongoing
Keep all records and document every financial impact of your injuries.

Crashes caused by fatigued truck drivers frequently result in high-severity injuries requiring extended treatment — surgeries, rehabilitation, and specialist follow-up over months or years. Every medical visit, prescription, adaptive equipment purchase, and day of missed work builds the damages case. Keep a running file of everything and share it with your attorney regularly. If you are unable to return to your previous occupation, vocational rehabilitation assessments and life care plans quantify that loss for presentation at trial or mediation.

What HOS violation crashes cause

A fatigued driver doesn't brake.
An 80,000-pound truck doesn't stop.

Fatigue crashes are often high-speed, no-brake collisions. The driver didn't see the hazard in time, or didn't see it at all. The injuries reflect the unmitigated force of that impact.

Traumatic Brain Injury
A fatigued driver who fails to brake produces an impact at or near full highway speed. The unattenuated collision forces transmitted through the vehicle to the occupant's skull and brain cause concussion, diffuse axonal injury, and severe TBI — including injuries that appear minor at first but result in lasting cognitive, behavioral, and neurological deficits.
Spinal Cord Injuries
High-speed rear-end and broadside impacts from a vehicle that failed to brake compress the cervical and thoracic spine with sudden, extreme force. Vertebral fractures, disc herniation, and spinal cord damage can produce partial or complete paralysis, requiring lifelong care, adaptive equipment, and home modifications that dramatically alter the victim's life and economic prospects.
Crush and Compartment Injuries
When an 80,000-pound truck strikes a passenger vehicle without braking, the weight disparity causes catastrophic intrusion into the passenger compartment. Occupants can be trapped by collapsed structural elements, leading to crush syndrome, compartment syndrome, and limb loss — injuries that require immediate surgical intervention and years of reconstructive care.
Multiple Fractures
The multi-directional forces in a high-speed truck impact — initial strike, secondary deceleration, and barrier contact — produce complex fracture patterns across the pelvis, femur, ribs, and upper extremities. Multiple concurrent fractures frequently require staged surgical procedures, prolonged immobilization, and extended rehabilitation before any functional recovery is possible.
Internal Organ Damage
Blunt abdominal trauma from vehicle deformation during a high-speed truck impact can rupture the spleen, liver, or kidneys, or cause mesenteric tears and internal hemorrhaging. These injuries are frequently not immediately apparent — the victim may walk away from the scene before life-threatening bleeding becomes evident — making a same-day emergency evaluation essential.
Burns and Fire Injuries
High-speed collisions carry an elevated fire risk: ruptured fuel tanks, friction ignition from dragging vehicle components, and — in hazmat loads — chemical exposure. Fatigued-driver crashes often involve higher impact speeds than other truck accidents precisely because the driver failed to brake, increasing both the probability of fuel system rupture and the severity of any resulting fire.
What they don't want you to know

The carrier's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Trucking companies carry large insurance policies and experienced defense teams. Here's what they typically argue in hours-of-service cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The driver's logs show legal hours — this wasn't a fatigue crash"
Paper logbooks were historically easy to falsify, and even ELD data can be manipulated in some systems. We subpoena the unfiltered ELD raw data, dispatch records, and GPS location history simultaneously — a driver who logged a 10-hour rest period but whose GPS shows movement during that window is precisely the kind of discrepancy that proves falsification and triggers punitive damages exposure.
Tactic 02
"Our ELD records don't show an HOS violation for this driver"
Carriers sometimes attribute hours to the wrong driver account, split driving logs across multiple duty statuses, or configure ELD systems to comply on paper while routing actual driving through unmonitored segments. Dispatch records, fuel receipts, toll records, and cell tower data often reconstruct the driver's actual timeline — revealing that what the ELD shows and what the driver actually did are two different things.
Tactic 03
"Delivery scheduling is standard industry practice — the carrier did nothing wrong"
The fact that a practice is common does not make it legal. Carriers that know their delivery windows require driving beyond legal limits and dispatch drivers anyway face direct liability — the scheduling pressure is the violation. Internal communications showing dispatch knew the driver was over hours but continued to assign loads are among the most powerful evidence in an HOS case, and carriers know it.
Tactic 04
"Fatigue can't be proven — there's no test for it at the scene"
Unlike alcohol, fatigue cannot be measured by a roadside test. Carriers use this to argue that drowsy driving is merely speculative. But ELD data showing the driver had been awake for 19 hours, eyewitness accounts of erratic driving before impact, and FMCSA's own research correlating hours to crash risk build the fatigue case without a blood test. The carrier's failure to prevent an HOS violation is the liability foundation, regardless of the driver's subjective state.

Weekly driving is capped at 60 hours over 7 consecutive days or 70 hours over 8 days, with a 34-hour restart provision available to reset the weekly cycle. These rules exist because federal crash research shows that fatigue impairs a driver's reaction time and hazard perception as severely as alcohol, and that crash risk more than doubles after the 8th consecutive hour behind the wheel.
A fatigued driver behind the wheel of an 80,000-pound truck is one of the most dangerous conditions on the highway. FMCSA research shows that crash risk more than doubles after the 8th hour of driving and continues rising with each additional hour. A driver who has exceeded the 11-hour limit or skipped the required 10-hour rest period has degraded reaction time, impaired lane-keeping, and reduced hazard recognition — yet continues operating at highway speed. HOS violations also frequently accompany other risk factors: drivers over their hours are more likely to skip pre-trip inspections, more likely to drive aggressively to make up lost time, and more likely to have been subjected to carrier scheduling pressure that incentivized the violation from the start.
An electronic logging device (ELD) is a federally mandated unit under 49 CFR Part 395 Subpart B that automatically records a commercial vehicle's engine hours, vehicle movement, miles driven, and location in real time. Unlike paper logbooks, which drivers could falsify, ELDs create a continuous, tamper-resistant record of actual driving time and rest periods. In an hours-of-service case, ELD data can prove the driver was behind the wheel beyond the 11-hour limit, had not completed a required 10-hour off-duty period, or skipped the mandatory 30-minute break. The carrier's dispatch and messaging records often reveal the scheduling pressure that caused the violation. A carrier that discovers a driver has exceeded HOS limits and continues to dispatch that driver faces liability for negligent entrustment. If ELD data reveals a pattern of systemic violations across the carrier's fleet, that evidence can support punitive damages under California Civil Code § 3294 for conscious disregard of known safety risks. Third-party logistics companies and shippers who impose delivery deadlines that cannot be met within legal driving windows may also share liability for creating the scheduling pressure.
