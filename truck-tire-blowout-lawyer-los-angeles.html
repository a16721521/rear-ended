Truck tire blowout crashes

When a carrier skips tire inspections, the road becomes the inspection — and other drivers pay the price.

A commercial truck tire blowout is not a random mechanical event. It is the predictable outcome of deferred maintenance — a carrier running tires past their service life, ignoring worn treads, operating with incorrect inflation, or reusing retreaded casings that no longer meet safety standards. When a tire fails at highway speed, the force is violent: rubber chunks the size of a car door shed across multiple lanes, the truck lurches and may swing toward adjacent vehicles, and the driver — often startled — may overcorrect in a way that triggers a secondary crash. Vehicles in the path have no warning.

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration regulations leave no ambiguity about who is responsible. Under 49 CFR § 396.3, carriers must systematically inspect, repair, and maintain all parts and accessories — including tires — and must not operate any vehicle with components in a condition likely to cause an accident or breakdown. Under 49 CFR § 392.7, drivers are required to inspect tire condition and inflation pressure before every trip. These are not aspirational guidelines — they are mandatory federal rules, and violating them creates direct liability for the carrier when a tire fails and causes injury.

Liability in a tire blowout case can extend beyond the carrier. If the tire had a manufacturing defect — a flaw in the casing, a failed retread bond, or a tread compound that separated under normal operating temperatures — the tire manufacturer and any retreading company may face product liability claims alongside the carrier's negligence. Third-party tire maintenance contractors, cargo loading companies that overloaded the trailer, and even the truck driver who signed off on a faulty pre-trip inspection may all bear a share of responsibility.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by your own percentage of fault — it is never cut off entirely. Step-by-step

What to do after a truck tire blowout crash.

Trucking carriers have rapid-response teams and legal departments that activate within hours. The blown tire, maintenance records, and black box data that prove neglect can disappear if you don't act fast.

1
At the scene
Call 911 and stay clear of tire debris on the roadway.

Blown truck tire debris scattered across the freeway creates a secondary hazard for other vehicles. Move to safety but stay at the scene. Get the responding officer's name, badge number, and report number. CHP typically responds to commercial truck incidents and will document tire debris field, position, and the condition of the remaining tires on the truck — critical for later inspection.

2
At the scene
Photograph the blown tire, debris field, and all vehicle damage.

Document the shredded tire or tread debris on the road, the condition of the truck's remaining tires, your vehicle's damage, and the debris scatter pattern. If the tire is still on the truck's rim, photograph the sidewall markings — the DOT code stamped on the sidewall encodes the manufacturer, plant, and week and year of manufacture, which is critical evidence for both product liability and age-based maintenance failure claims.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved before the carrier's team replaces or discards the tire.

The blown tire is the most critical piece of physical evidence in your case — and carriers routinely replace or discard blown tires before litigation if they are not put on formal notice. A spoliation letter sent immediately preserves the tire, all remaining tires on the truck, maintenance and inspection logs, ELD data, pre-trip inspection reports, and driver qualification files. Trucking companies often dispatch rapid-response teams within hours of a serious crash to manage evidence and the scene narrative.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment even if you feel uninjured.

Tire blowout crashes can involve high-speed swerving, secondary collisions, and debris strikes. Spinal injuries, internal bleeding, and traumatic brain injuries frequently present with delayed symptoms that worsen over the following hours and days. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record that ties your injuries to this specific crash — without it, the carrier's insurer will argue your injuries were pre-existing or caused by something else entirely.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the carrier's insurer.

The trucking company's insurer will contact you quickly — sometimes the same day — framing the call as routine paperwork. Their goal is to get a statement on record that shifts fault: suggesting you were in the truck's blind spot, following too closely, or had time to avoid the debris. These statements become evidence used against you at trial. Let your attorney handle all contact with the carrier and its insurer from the first call.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document every day of lost income.

Tire blowout crash injuries — particularly those involving secondary collisions at highway speed — often require extended treatment: surgeries, imaging, physical therapy, and specialist follow-ups over months or years. Keep a file of every medical visit, prescription, therapy session, and employer notice of missed work. Share everything with your attorney regularly. Damages that aren't documented are damages that won't be recovered.

What tire blowout crashes cause

Debris at highway speed.
A truck losing control. The injuries follow.

A truck tire blowout can trigger a sudden swerve, secondary crash, or debris field that spans multiple lanes. Vehicles struck by a tire chunk or hit by a swerving truck have little warning and less time to react.

Traumatic Brain Injury
Sudden impacts from a truck swerve or debris strike, or secondary collisions when the truck loses control, can produce concussion, diffuse axonal injury, and severe TBI even in belted occupants. Neurological symptoms — cognitive changes, persistent headache, balance problems — frequently appear days after the crash and are often misattributed to stress rather than brain injury without prompt imaging.
Spinal Cord Injuries
The violent lateral or rear force of being struck by a swerving truck, or the whiplash and compression from a high-speed secondary collision, can fracture vertebrae and damage the spinal cord. Incomplete and complete spinal cord injuries resulting from tire blowout crashes can produce partial or permanent paralysis and require lifelong medical management and adaptive equipment.
Lacerations and Penetrating Injuries
Chunks of commercial truck tread can weigh 50 pounds or more and separate at speeds exceeding 65 mph. When a tread slab strikes a windshield or enters a passenger compartment, it can cause severe penetrating wounds, facial lacerations, and eye injuries. Even smaller debris fragments striking at highway speed can cause significant soft-tissue damage to exposed skin.
Crush Injuries
When a truck loses control after a blowout and strikes another vehicle directly, the weight differential — up to 80,000 pounds against a 4,000-pound passenger car — produces catastrophic compartment intrusion. Crush injuries to the lower extremities, pelvis, and thorax can result in compartment syndrome, organ damage, and the need for emergency extrication and amputation.
Multiple Fractures
The high-speed, multi-directional forces of a tire blowout crash — debris impact, sudden swerve, secondary collision — produce complex fracture patterns across multiple body regions. Femur, pelvis, clavicle, rib, and upper extremity fractures frequently occur together, requiring multiple surgical interventions, internal fixation hardware, and extended inpatient rehabilitation.
Internal Organ Damage
Blunt abdominal trauma from a truck strike or high-speed secondary collision can rupture the spleen, liver, or kidneys, or cause mesenteric tears and internal hemorrhaging. These injuries are frequently not immediately visible and require emergency CT imaging to diagnose — hours without treatment can be life-threatening. Same-day emergency evaluation is essential even without obvious external injuries.
What they don't want you to know

The carrier's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Trucking companies carry large insurance policies and experienced defense teams. Here's what they typically argue in tire blowout cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The blowout was sudden — the driver had no warning and couldn't react"
Carriers present tire blowouts as unforeseeable emergencies to shield themselves from maintenance liability. Tire maintenance records and pre-trip inspection logs usually tell a different story — worn tread noted weeks earlier and not replaced, inflation pressure checks skipped for months, or a retreaded casing put back in service beyond its rated mileage. A blowout that looks sudden in the moment was built up over miles of deferred maintenance.
Tactic 02
"The tire was within legal load and pressure specifications at its last check"
Carriers argue technical compliance to avoid responsibility for tires they knew were degrading. Legal specifications set a floor, not a ceiling for carrier diligence — a tire that was technically within limits at the last logged inspection but showed visible sidewall cracking, age-related dry rot, or tread approaching the minimum still should have been replaced. Inspection logs that show the carrier saw a deteriorating tire and approved it for another run establish the negligence directly.
Tactic 03
"Your vehicle was following too closely and had time to avoid the debris"
This argument puts the crash back on the driver behind the truck. Tread debris at highway speed travels faster than reaction time allows — studies show drivers have less than a second to react to a debris field that appears at 65 mph. The carrier's obligation to maintain tires so they don't shed tread in the first place cannot be excused by arguing that the resulting debris could have been avoided.
Tactic 04
"The tire manufacturer is responsible — we just ran the tire they built"
Carriers try to redirect the entire case to the tire manufacturer to escape their own maintenance obligations. The two theories of liability are independent and can both be pursued simultaneously — a defective tire that also wasn't inspected on schedule means both the manufacturer and the carrier are liable. We pursue both tracks in parallel to maximize your recovery and prevent either defendant from pointing only at the other.

Questions & answers

Truck tire blowout accident FAQ.

Commercial truck tires blow out for reasons rooted in maintenance failures, not random bad luck. The most common causes include running tires past their service life — treads worn below safe minimums or sidewalls cracked from age — and operating tires with incorrect inflation pressure or under excessive load. Retreaded tires, which are common in commercial fleets, can delaminate and shed tread at highway speed if the retread process was done improperly or if the casing was reused beyond its safe service limit. FMCSA regulations under 49 CFR § 396.3 require carriers to systematically inspect and maintain tires, and 49 CFR § 392.7 requires drivers to inspect tire condition and inflation before every trip. When carriers skip these checks or run tires past their service limits, a blowout becomes predictable — not an accident.
Multiple parties may be liable. The motor carrier is primarily responsible under federal regulations requiring tire maintenance and inspection. The truck driver may be liable for failing to conduct required pre-trip inspections or continuing to drive on a visibly deteriorating tire. If the tire had a manufacturing defect — a flaw in the casing, bead, or tread compound — the tire manufacturer and distributor may face product liability claims independent of the carrier's negligence. Third-party tire maintenance contractors who performed retreading or repairs may be liable if their work was substandard. And if an overloaded trailer caused a tire to fail under excessive weight, the cargo loading company may share liability alongside the carrier.
The blown tire itself is the most important piece of physical evidence — it must be preserved immediately, because carriers have been known to discard or replace tires before litigation. Tire maintenance and inspection records, required under 49 CFR § 396.3, show whether the carrier inspected tires on schedule and flagged and corrected any problems. Pre-trip inspection logs reveal whether the driver performed the required check and noted the tire's condition before departure. Electronic logging device (ELD) and black box data show the truck's speed, any sudden maneuvers, and the sequence of events in the seconds before the blowout. Tire age, mileage, load rating documentation, and retread service records establish whether the tire was operating within safe parameters.
Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration regulations impose specific maintenance obligations on carriers. Under 49 CFR § 396.3, carriers must systematically inspect, repair, and maintain all parts and accessories — including tires — and ensure no commercial vehicle is operated with components likely to cause an accident or breakdown. Under 49 CFR § 392.7, drivers must complete a pre-trip inspection that covers tire condition and inflation before each run. When a carrier fails to document timely tire inspections, runs tires past their tread depth minimums, uses retreads that fail inspection standards, or allows overloaded trailers that stress tires beyond their rated capacity, the resulting blowout is the direct consequence of federal regulatory violations. This creates a direct path to carrier liability separate from any product defect claim against the tire manufacturer.

Product liability claims require that the tire be preserved as evidence immediately — before it is repaired, replaced, or discarded.
