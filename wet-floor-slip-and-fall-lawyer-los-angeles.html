Wet floor premises liability

How long was the hazard there? The inspection log tells us.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of ordinary care on every property owner — commercial retailers, grocery stores, restaurants, and residential landlords alike. When a wet or slippery floor condition exists and a property owner fails to address it within a reasonable time, they are liable for the injuries that result. The law does not require proof that the owner knew about the spill — only that they should have known through reasonable inspection and maintenance.

The critical legal standard is "actual or constructive notice." Actual notice means someone told the owner or their employees about the hazard. Constructive notice — far more common — means the condition existed long enough that the owner should have discovered it through routine inspection. A spill that sat in a grocery store aisle for 40 minutes, a mopped floor re-opened to foot traffic before it was dry, condensation pooling under a refrigeration unit that was never reported — these are constructive notice situations. Inspection logs, cleaning schedules, spill-incident reports, and employee testimony establish when the hazard first appeared and whether anyone checked the area before you fell. These records are our primary targets.

The "Caution: wet floor" sign defense is widely misunderstood. A cone is evidence that the owner had actual notice of the hazard — which actually helps the liability case. It does not excuse the owner for failing to clean the floor within a reasonable time. A sign posted 30 seconds after your fall, positioned around a corner, or placed alongside a two-hour-old spill does not absolve the property owner of responsibility. Under California law, the duty is to eliminate the hazard — not merely to warn about it.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by your own percentage of fault — it is never eliminated entirely. The deadline to file is two years under CCP § 335.1, or six months if the fall occurred on government property under Government Code § 911.2. But the practical deadline is much shorter — surveillance footage is overwritten in 24–72 hours. Contact us today before that window closes.

Step-by-step

What to do after a wet floor fall.

Property owners and their insurers move fast. Surveillance footage is overwritten in 24–72 hours. The wet floor may be cleaned up within minutes. Here is how to protect your claim before that happens.

1
At the scene
Report the fall to the property manager and get an incident report.

Notify whoever is in charge — store manager, restaurant supervisor, building staff — and ask them to create an incident report before you leave. Get a copy. This creates an official record the property owner cannot later deny, locks in the date and time, and forces an employee to document the condition. Do not say you are "fine" or "okay." Adrenaline masks pain and you do not yet know the full extent of your injuries.

2
At the scene
Photograph the hazard and the area before it is cleaned up.

Photograph the wet floor, any liquid or cleaning residue present, the placement of any warning cones or signs (or the absence of them), and the broader area including nearby employees or activity. Note the exact time. Photograph from multiple angles — close up on the wet surface, wide shot showing the context. Note whether signage was present before your fall or placed afterward. Property owners frequently clean up hazards within minutes. The photographs you take before that happens are often the most important evidence in the case.

3
At the scene
Get witness contact information before they leave.

Anyone who saw you fall, saw the wet floor before you arrived, or heard employees discuss the condition is a potential witness. Names and phone numbers — get them before people disperse. Witnesses who are not documented at the scene become very difficult to locate later, and their accounts of what the floor looked like before your fall can establish how long the hazard was present.

4
Same day
See a doctor — do not wait.

Hip fractures, spinal injuries, and traumatic brain injuries from falls are not always immediately apparent. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this specific fall. A gap in treatment — even 48 hours — becomes an argument that the fall was not responsible for your injuries. Emergency rooms and urgent care clinics will document the mechanism of injury (a fall on a wet floor) in the records, which is essential evidence.

5
Same day
Preserve your clothing and footwear exactly as they are.

Your shoes and clothing are evidence. Property owners and insurers routinely argue that inappropriate footwear contributed to the fall. Do not wash or discard them. Bag them and set them aside. If possible, photograph your footwear at the scene before removing them — an image of the shoe soles in context is more persuasive than a photo taken later at home. The presence or absence of slip-resistant soles and the condition of the heel all become relevant if the owner raises a footwear defense.

6
As soon as possible
Call Law Dog before giving any statement to the property's insurer.

The property owner's insurer will contact you quickly — often within 24 hours — framing it as a routine information call. Their goal is to get a recorded statement before you have legal representation. Do not give one. Do not sign anything. Surveillance footage showing the wet floor existed before you arrived — often the single most powerful piece of evidence — is overwritten in 24 to 72 hours. The sooner we send a preservation demand, the more evidence we lock down.

What wet floor falls cause

A fall on a hard floor
is never trivial.

Wet floor falls happen fast with no warning. The body hits a hard surface with full force — no crumple zone, no airbag. Insurers minimize these injuries. We document every consequence.

Hip Fractures
The most severe and common serious injury from wet floor falls, particularly in older adults. A lateral fall onto a hard floor transmits force directly to the hip. Displaced fractures often require total hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation — with elevated risk of secondary complications that can be life-threatening.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Head contact with a hard floor during a backward or sideways fall can cause concussion, skull fracture, subdural hematoma, or diffuse axonal injury. Cognitive changes, memory impairment, and personality shifts may not appear for days after the fall — making same-day medical evaluation critical even when the victim initially seems uninjured.
Spinal Injuries
A backward fall compresses the lumbar spine on impact, causing herniated discs, vertebral fractures, or spinal cord damage with partial or permanent neurological effects. Adrenaline at the scene often masks the severity — victims who walked away from the scene have later required spinal surgery when full symptoms emerged over the following days.
Broken Wrists
The instinct to break a fall with outstretched hands causes wrist fractures — often comminuted (shattered) fractures requiring open reduction and internal fixation with hardware. Grip strength and fine motor function may be permanently reduced, affecting the ability to work and perform daily tasks indefinitely after the injury.
Knee & Shoulder Injuries
Twisting during a slip or impact during a fall can tear the ACL, meniscus, or rotator cuff. These injuries frequently require surgical repair and extended physical therapy. Full functional recovery is not guaranteed — many victims have lasting instability, chronic pain, and restrictions on activity that affect employment and quality of life long after treatment ends.
Ankle Fractures & Soft Tissue
Slipping on a wet floor commonly involves ankle fractures with ligament involvement, as well as sprains, tendon tears, and muscle damage that insurers aggressively minimize. These injuries cause lasting pain and joint instability that affects daily function — and they do not always appear on standard X-rays, requiring MRI to fully document.
What they don't want you to know

The property owner's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Grocery chains, restaurant groups, and commercial property owners carry large liability policies and experienced defense teams. Here is what they typically argue in wet floor cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The spill just happened — we had no notice."
This is the most common defense in wet floor cases. The property owner claims the spill occurred moments before you fell, so there was no time to address it. Inspection logs and cleaning schedules tell a different story. If the property cannot produce a log showing the area was checked within a reasonable time before your fall — or if the log shows the last inspection was hours earlier — constructive notice is established. Surveillance footage showing the spill before your fall is often decisive.
Tactic 02
"We had a 'Caution: wet floor' sign in place."
A warning sign does not eliminate liability — it confirms it. The sign proves the owner had actual notice of the hazard. The question becomes whether the sign was adequate: was it visible from all approach directions? Was it positioned before you entered the wet area? Was the floor cleaned within a reasonable time after the sign was placed? A cone left next to a two-hour-old spill is not adequate care under California law. We obtain footage and employee statements that establish the sign's placement and timing.
Tactic 03
"You weren't watching where you were going."
This is a comparative fault argument — not a complete defense. Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault, not eliminated. And inattention on your part does not excuse the property owner's duty to maintain safe floors. When a floor is wet without adequate signage, or when a hazard is in a predictable traffic path, the owner's failure — not your attention level — is the primary cause of the fall.
Tactic 04
"Our floor surface meets all applicable standards."
Code compliance is a minimum floor — not a defense against negligence. A floor surface that is adequate when dry can become unreasonably dangerous when wet. The duty of care under Civil Code § 1714 requires property owners to address the actual condition of their floors at all times, not merely to select compliant materials at installation. A floor that passes code on paper but becomes dangerously slippery when exposed to condensation, cleaning chemicals, or spills is not reasonably safe — and the owner knew it.

Grocery stores and restaurants account for a disproportionate share of these incidents because liquid is constantly present in these environments. What all these scenarios share is that the property owner either created the hazardous condition or had enough time to discover and address it before someone was injured.
