Inadequate lighting injuries

When the light went out, the owner's duty didn't go with it.

California Civil Code § 1714 imposes a duty of reasonable care on every property owner in the management of their property — and lighting is a property condition. When a bulb burns out in a parking garage stairwell, a commercial corridor, or an apartment walkway and the owner fails to replace it, they have created a hazardous condition on the premises. The longer the lighting has been out, the stronger the constructive notice argument: any property with a basic maintenance inspection routine would have discovered and remedied it. We subpoena maintenance records and work orders to establish exactly how long the owner knew.

Building codes and OSHA lighting standards are powerful evidence of what "adequate" means. The Los Angeles Municipal Code and the California Building Code set minimum foot-candle levels for parking structures, stairwells, corridors, and exit routes. When a parking garage falls below one foot-candle at ground level, or a stairwell lacks emergency backup lighting, those code violations are direct evidence that the owner's duty was breached. But code compliance is not a safe harbor — California courts evaluate whether lighting was sufficient for a reasonably careful person to safely navigate the area, not merely whether the property met the lowest legal threshold.

The "open and obvious" defense fails in the dark. A hazard that would be immediately visible in normal lighting — a step-down at a ramp's end, a raised threshold at a doorway, a wet surface, an uneven floor transition — can be completely invisible in near-darkness. California courts have consistently held that the open-and-obvious doctrine does not apply when inadequate lighting was the reason the hazard could not be detected. The very condition the owner failed to correct is what prevented the visitor from seeing what the owner now claims was obvious.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated entirely. If the property is government-owned, Government Code § 911.2 gives you just six months to file an administrative claim. Contact us today — maintenance records and surveillance footage that prove how long the lighting had been out must be preserved before the property owner has a chance to lose them.

Step-by-step

What to do after an inadequate lighting injury.

Property owners move quickly to replace bulbs and repair lighting after an incident — eliminating the very evidence that proves their negligence. The maintenance records and footage that show how long the light was out can disappear just as fast.

1
At the scene
Photograph the lighting conditions before they change anything.

Document the dark area from the perspective of where you were walking when you fell — what you could and could not see. Photograph the burned-out or missing fixture, the specific hazard that caused your fall, and any warning signs that were or were not present. If you can safely attempt to operate a light switch or test a fixture, document that it doesn't work. Property owners frequently replace bulbs within hours of an incident. The lighting conditions at the moment of your fall are the most important thing to capture.

2
At the scene
Report the incident to whoever manages the property.

Notify the store manager, building superintendent, parking attendant, hotel security, or property management — whoever is on duty. Ask them to create an incident report and get a copy before you leave. What the property manager writes in that report becomes important evidence: a report acknowledging a burned-out fixture effectively establishes notice. Never say you are "fine" or "okay." You don't know yet, and those words will follow your claim.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney to send a preservation letter immediately.

Maintenance records and work orders showing when the lighting problem was first reported and how long it went unaddressed are the most powerful evidence in an inadequate lighting case. Surveillance footage — which may show how long the light had been out, or employees passing through the same dark corridor — is typically overwritten within 24–72 hours. A preservation letter sent immediately puts the property owner on legal notice to retain this evidence. Owners who destroy records after receiving formal notice face significant legal consequences.

4
Same day
Get medical treatment — even if you think you're fine.

Falls in dark environments often produce head injuries, spinal injuries, and orthopedic injuries. Adrenaline at the time of the fall masks pain, and significant injuries frequently become apparent only hours or days later. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this specific incident. A gap in treatment — even 48 hours — becomes an insurer's argument that the injuries weren't caused by the fall or weren't serious. Don't give them that opening.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the property's insurer.

The property owner's insurer will contact you quickly — often within 48 hours — framing the call as a routine follow-up. Their goal is to get you on record before you understand your rights. They will ask about your familiarity with the area, your footwear, the lighting you noticed, and whether you had been drinking. Every answer becomes ammunition for a comparative fault argument. Let your attorney handle all communication with the property owner and their insurer.

6
Ongoing
Keep all medical records and document lost income.

Inadequate lighting injuries — particularly head injuries, spinal injuries, and hip fractures — often require extended treatment over months or years. Every medical visit, prescription, specialist referral, and therapy session builds the damages case. Document every day of work missed and every activity you can no longer perform. Share this information with your attorney regularly, because the damages evidence determines what your recovery is ultimately worth.

What inadequate lighting causes

No warning. No time to brace.
The fall is uncontrolled.

Victims falling in near-darkness have no time to prepare. They don't see the hazard coming — so they don't brace, shift weight, or adjust. The injuries reflect that full uncontrolled impact.

Traumatic Brain Injury
No warning means no defensive bracing. An uncontrolled fall in darkness produces direct head impact with the ground, a floor surface, or a fixed object. Concussion, skull fracture, subdural hematoma, and diffuse axonal injury are all possible. TBI symptoms — cognitive fog, memory difficulty, personality changes — may not fully appear for days or weeks after the fall.
Hip Fractures
A direct lateral fall in darkness often produces hip impact without the partial protection that comes from anticipating a fall. Hip fractures are most serious in older adults and frequently require total hip replacement surgery, extended hospitalization, and months of rehabilitation, with elevated risk of secondary complications and permanent mobility loss.
Spinal Injuries
Backward falls — common when a victim trips on an unseen hazard — transmit force through the spine on impact. Lumbar vertebral fractures, disc herniation, and spinal cord damage with neurological effects are all possible outcomes. Chronic back and leg pain following inadequate lighting falls can be permanent, with significant effects on employment and daily function.
Wrist and Forearm Fractures
Even in darkness, the defensive bracing reflex is nearly universal. Outstretched hands and wrists absorb the impact force, producing Colles fractures and distal radius fractures that frequently require surgical repair with hardware. Grip strength and fine motor function may be permanently reduced — a significant loss for anyone who works with their hands.
Knee Injuries
Twisting or falling on an unseen surface change can tear the ACL, MCL, or meniscus. These injuries frequently require surgery and months of physical therapy. Long-term outcomes are variable — some patients regain full function, many do not — and the permanent effect on mobility, exercise capacity, and daily life is often significant.
Facial and Dental Injuries
An uncontrolled fall where the victim has no warning often results in facial impact with the ground or a fixed object. Fractured orbital bones, cheekbones, and dental injuries are common outcomes that carry significant treatment costs. In visible locations, permanent facial disfigurement adds a non-economic damages component that is separate from physical pain and disability.
What they don't want you to know

The property owner's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Property owners carry liability insurance and experienced defense teams. Here's what they typically argue in inadequate lighting cases — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"The lighting met minimum code requirements"
After an incident, carriers often commission post-incident lighting measurements showing the property met minimum foot-candle levels. Minimum code compliance is not exculpatory — California courts evaluate whether lighting was sufficient for a reasonably careful person to safely navigate the area, not whether it hit the lowest legal threshold. We retain lighting engineers who measure actual conditions and testify to what a visitor would have been able to see in those specific conditions.
Tactic 02
"You should have been more careful in dim conditions"
This argument assigns blame to the victim for not adapting to the property owner's negligence. Under California's pure comparative fault rule, this argument can reduce — but never eliminate — your recovery. The more powerful counter is the evidence itself: a burned-out fixture unreplaced for weeks, no warning signs, and a hazard that was completely invisible in the surrounding darkness. The property owner's failure to maintain lighting was the proximate cause of your inability to see the hazard.
Tactic 03
"The bulb had just burned out — we had no notice"
A burned-out bulb in a parking garage stairwell that goes unnoticed by property management is constructive notice: any property with a basic maintenance inspection routine should discover it within days. We subpoena maintenance logs and work order systems to establish exactly how long the lighting was defective. Prior incident reports from other visitors to the same location are particularly powerful — if someone fell in the same dark corridor months earlier, ignorance is not a defense.
Tactic 04
"You were familiar with the area and knew it was dark"
Familiarity with a property does not eliminate an owner's duty to maintain adequate lighting. A tenant who has used an apartment stairwell for two years still has the right to expect adequate light — they cannot be expected to avoid common areas entirely because the landlord won't replace bulbs. Familiarity may be raised as a comparative fault argument, but it is not a complete defense and courts treat it that way.

Questions & answers

Inadequate lighting injury FAQ.

California courts evaluate lighting adequacy by examining whether the lighting was sufficient for a reasonably careful person to safely navigate the area. The Los Angeles Municipal Code, California Building Code, and OSHA standards set minimum foot-candle levels for parking structures, stairwells, corridors, and exit routes — falling below these thresholds makes the negligence case difficult to dispute. But code compliance is not a safe harbor: owners can be liable even when lighting meets minimum levels if it was insufficient to make hazards visible in actual conditions. A burned-out bulb in an otherwise adequate system, inconsistent lighting that creates blind spots, and seasonal changes that affect natural light are all relevant to whether lighting was adequate on the night in question.
Property owners are liable under Civil Code § 1714 both when they created the dangerous condition and when they had constructive notice — meaning they should have known through reasonable inspection and maintenance. For lighting defects, constructive notice attaches quickly: a burned-out bulb in a commercial corridor or parking structure is something any maintenance routine should catch within days. We subpoena maintenance records, work orders, and inspection logs to establish how long the lighting had been defective before your fall. Incident reports from prior falls in the same location are particularly powerful — if another person fell in the same dark corridor months earlier, the property owner cannot claim ignorance. Surveillance footage of employees using the same area while the light was out also establishes notice.
The "open and obvious" defense is severely limited in inadequate lighting cases. The doctrine holds that property owners are not liable for hazards a reasonable person would have seen and avoided. But this premise breaks down when darkness prevents seeing. California courts have consistently held that a hazard that might be visible in daylight is not open and obvious when inadequate lighting makes it impossible to detect. A step-down, raised threshold, floor transition, or wet surface that is readily visible in normal lighting can be completely invisible in near-darkness. Owners who argue "open and obvious" in a darkness case face a significant credibility problem: the very condition they failed to correct is what prevented the visitor from seeing the hazard they are now calling obvious.
Parking garages and surface parking lots account for a large share of inadequate lighting injury claims — they are typically managed by owners with cost-control incentives who defer lighting maintenance. Apartment and condominium stairwells, exterior walkways, and laundry facilities are another common location, where landlords owe tenants and their guests a duty to maintain adequate lighting in common areas. Nightclub and bar exits, where patrons transitioning from lit interiors to dark exteriors can miss steps or curbs, generate significant claims. Commercial corridors and loading areas where shift workers walk in early-morning or late-night darkness are also frequent sites. Government property — city parking structures, parks, transit facilities — is a distinct category with a six-month filing deadline under Government Code § 911.2.
Yes. California follows pure comparative fault under Civil Code § 1431.2. Even if a jury finds you were partially at fault — for example, that you were moving faster than was prudent in dim conditions — your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault, not eliminated. If damages are $400,000 and you are found 25% at fault, you recover $300,000. Property owners routinely argue that victims should have been more careful in areas they knew were dim. This is a comparative fault argument, not a complete defense. The property owner's failure to maintain adequate lighting is always the primary causal factor when a lighting defect is at issue.
Two years from the date of the injury under California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 for claims against a private property owner — a commercial landlord, retailer, apartment owner, or business. Six months under Government Code § 911.2 if the fall occurred on government property — a city parking structure, public park, county building, or government transit facility. The six-month deadline is an absolute bar: missing it permanently extinguishes the claim regardless of how severe the injuries are. But the practical deadline is shorter still: maintenance records and surveillance footage showing how long the lighting had been defective can disappear quickly. Time limit

Two years to file. Maintenance records disappear faster.

California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years from the injury date to file a personal injury lawsuit. But the evidence that wins inadequate lighting cases — maintenance logs, work orders, bulb replacement records, and surveillance footage showing how long the fixture had been out — can be overwritten or destroyed if a spoliation letter isn't sent immediately. If the property is government-owned, the deadline is six months under Government Code § 911.2. The sooner we start, the more evidence we lock down.
