Rideshare accidents

The app status at the moment of the crash changes everything.

Uber and Lyft accidents are legally distinct from ordinary car accidents because they involve layered insurance — a combination of the driver's personal policy and the company's commercial coverage — and which layer applies depends entirely on what the driver's app was doing at the exact moment of impact. Most victims have no idea this matters, and both the driver's personal insurer and the rideshare company's claims team have every incentive to keep it confusing.

California's Public Utilities Commission regulates Transportation Network Companies (TNCs) under PUC § 5430 et seq. and mandates specific insurance requirements for each operating period. When a driver is actively transporting a passenger or en route to pick one up, a $1 million commercial liability policy activates — covering passengers, third parties, and every other injured party regardless of fault structure.

If you were a passenger in the Uber or Lyft, you are covered under that $1M policy as a direct claimant. If you were in another vehicle, a cyclist, or a pedestrian struck by an on-trip rideshare driver, the same $1M policy applies to your claim. The driver's personal insurance is generally irrelevant — and will deny coverage for commercial use anyway.

What makes these cases complex is corporate resistance. Uber and Lyft steer injured users toward their in-app support system, designed for minor complaints — not catastrophic injuries. They dispute trip status, claim the driver was off the app, and move quickly to close cases before victims understand what they're entitled to. The three coverage periods

Which policy applies
depends on one thing.

The driver's app status at the moment of impact determines which insurance tier covers the crash. This is the first thing we establish in every rideshare case.

Period 0
App is off — driver not working
Personal policy only
The driver's personal auto insurance applies. Most personal policies exclude commercial use — so if the driver was actually between rides and claims their app was off, their personal insurer may still deny. We subpoena app records to determine true status.
Period 1
App on — available, no ride yet
$50K / $100K / $25K
California PUC § 5433 requires Uber and Lyft to carry contingency coverage during Period 1: $50,000 per person, $100,000 per accident, $25,000 property damage. This only activates if the driver's personal policy denies — which it almost always does for commercial use.
Periods 2 & 3
Ride accepted through drop-off
$1,000,000
The full commercial liability policy is active from the moment the driver accepts a ride request until the passenger is dropped off. This $1M policy covers passengers, pedestrians, cyclists, and occupants of other vehicles — regardless of how fault is ultimately allocated.

Step-by-step

What to do after an Uber or Lyft crash.

One action matters more than any other in a rideshare crash: screenshot the app immediately. Everything else can be reconstructed. The driver's app status at the moment of impact cannot be recreated if it's closed.

Free case review →
1
Immediately
Screenshot the Uber or Lyft app right now.

Before anything else — before calling 911 if you can do it in seconds — screenshot the app on your phone and the driver's phone if possible. The app status (trip active, en route, available) is the single most important piece of evidence in a rideshare case. It determines which of three insurance tiers applies. Drivers sometimes close the app immediately after a crash. Capture it now.

2
Immediately
Get your trip receipt emailed to you.

If you were a passenger, go into the Uber or Lyft app and request your trip receipt immediately. It will contain the trip ID, driver information, pickup and drop-off timestamps, and the route — all of which independently establish that an active trip was in progress at the time of the crash.

3
At the scene
Call 911 and document everything.

Police report, photographs of all vehicles, driver's license and insurance, witness names and numbers. In a rideshare crash, also photograph the vehicle's TNC license plate sticker (required in California), the Uber/Lyft trade dress on the dashboard, and the driver's app screen if it's still visible.

4
Critical
Do not use Uber or Lyft's in-app support to handle your injury claim.

Both companies have in-app support flows for post-crash incidents. These are designed for minor vehicle damage and driver complaints — not serious personal injury. Engaging their support process and accepting any form of payment or credit constitutes a release in some contexts. Any injury claim should be handled as a formal insurance claim, not through the app. Do not accept vouchers, credits, or any payment through the platform for your injuries.

5
Within 24 hours
Seek medical attention.

Even if you feel shaken but functional, see a doctor the same day or next morning. Rideshare crashes involve the same injury profiles as any other car crash — whiplash, TBI, soft tissue, internal injuries — and the same delayed onset applies. A medical record created within 24 hours of the crash is far stronger than one created a week later when symptoms intensified.

6
Before you speak to anyone
Call us before talking to any insurer.

Uber's and Lyft's insurance carriers are professional claims operations whose job is to settle quickly and cheaply. They will call within days. Do not give a recorded statement, do not confirm details of the crash, and do not accept any offer without legal review. We take over all insurer communication from the moment you retain us.

What they don't want you to know

How Uber, Lyft, and their insurers fight rideshare claims.

Rideshare companies and their insurers have refined their claims defense strategy over a decade of litigation. Know what's coming.

Tactic 01
Disputing the Driver's App Status
"The driver had logged off before the crash" — even when the evidence says otherwise. Without legal process compelling Uber or Lyft to produce their server-side trip logs, this claim is difficult to counter immediately. We compel production of those records in discovery and have successfully established on-trip status in cases where the company initially claimed the driver was offline.
Tactic 02
Independent Contractor Shield
Uber and Lyft classify drivers as independent contractors — which under most circumstances limits corporate vicarious liability. They invoke this aggressively to avoid direct suits. We evaluate corporate liability independently: negligent background check failures, failure to deactivate known dangerous drivers, defective app design that created driver distraction, and facts suggesting an employment relationship rather than contractor status.
Tactic 03
In-App "Resolution" Steering
Both companies route injured users through their app's support system — offering vouchers, credits, or small payments through a process that was designed for trip disputes, not serious injury. Any payment accepted through this process may be treated as a full and final release. The in-app system is not a legal claims process. We take over all communication with the company the moment you retain us.
Tactic 04
Fast Low Settlement Before Diagnosis
Uber and Lyft's commercial insurers move quickly precisely because they know injured people are anxious, unrepresented, and often unaware of the $1M policy limits. Early offers are calibrated to sound reasonable while being a fraction of the actual value once full medical care is accounted for. You cannot reopen a claim after signing a release. Never settle a rideshare injury without a complete medical picture and legal review.

Time limit

Two years. But Uber and Lyft's claims teams move in days.

California law gives you two years from the crash to file a personal injury lawsuit (CCP § 335.1). If any government vehicle was involved, that drops to six months. But the practical deadline is far shorter: rideshare company insurers make early settlement contact within days, camera evidence disappears within 72 hours, and medical documentation windows close fast.
