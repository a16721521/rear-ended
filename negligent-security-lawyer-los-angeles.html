Negligent security claims

The property didn't create the danger. It ignored it.

Under Civil Code § 1714, every property owner and operator in California owes visitors a duty of ordinary care — and that duty extends beyond spills and broken stairs to protecting people from foreseeable criminal acts by third parties. When a shooting, assault, or robbery happens on a property with broken locks, non-functioning cameras, or no security presence despite a known crime problem, the property can be held liable alongside — or instead of — an attacker who is never identified or never has assets to pursue.

California courts require a foreseeability analysis before imposing a duty to provide security: how predictable was this type of crime, and how burdensome would it have been to prevent it? Prior similar crimes on the property or in the immediate area are the clearest evidence of foreseeability, but courts have also found liability where the security failure itself was severe enough — a gate propped permanently open, a security contract that lapsed and was never renewed — that even a thinner crime history was enough to put the owner on notice.

We build these cases around the specific failures: broken locks and access gates, security cameras that weren't operational or monitored, guards cut from the budget despite a documented history of crime, and inadequate lighting in parking areas and stairwells — a related but distinct claim covered on our inadequate lighting page. Liability patterns differ by property type: nightclubs and bars face scrutiny over bouncer staffing and intoxicated-patron handling, apartment complexes and hotels over access control, and parking structures over camera coverage and guard patrols.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), your recovery is reduced only by any fault attributable to you — it is never eliminated. Step-by-step

What to do after a negligent security incident.

Property owners and their insurers move fast to fix the security failure and destroy the footage that proves it existed. Here's how to protect your claim.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Call 911 and get an official police report.

An attack on someone else's property should always generate a police report with a case number — this becomes your primary record of what happened and when. Ask for the responding officer's name and badge number, and request the report number so it can be obtained later. This report also becomes the first entry in the crime history that can establish foreseeability.

2
At the scene
Photograph the security failures, not just your injuries.

Document broken locks, propped-open gates, cameras that appear disconnected or non-functional, unlit or poorly lit areas, and the absence of any security guard or attendant. These conditions are often quietly repaired within days of an attack — photos taken before that happens are frequently the most persuasive evidence in the case.

3
Within hours
Get an attorney involved before the footage is overwritten.

Most commercial surveillance systems overwrite footage on a 24-to-72-hour cycle. Having an attorney send a preservation letter immediately locks down surveillance video, access logs, and any incident reports the property's own staff generated before management has a chance to lose, misplace, or record over them.

4
Same day
Get emergency medical treatment and follow through.

Gunshot wounds, stab wounds, and blunt force trauma require immediate treatment, but the psychological injury from a violent attack — acute stress, anxiety, PTSD — deserves the same attention. A same-day medical and psychological evaluation creates the causation record linking your injuries to this specific incident.

5
Within days
Do not give a recorded statement to the property's insurer.

The property's liability insurer will often contact you quickly, framing the call as routine. Their real goal is to get you to say the area was well lit, that you weren't paying attention, or that security seemed adequate — statements that get used to shift blame later. Let your attorney handle all communication with the insurer.

6
Ongoing
Request the property's crime history and keep your records.

We file California Public Records Act requests for police computer-aided dispatch (CAD) logs and prior incident reports tied to the property and surrounding block — the core evidence of foreseeability. Meanwhile, keep every medical bill, therapy record, and note about missed work, and share it with your attorney regularly.

What negligent security attacks cause

Violence on someone else's
property is catastrophic.

Negligent security cases involve attacks a functioning lock, camera, or guard could have prevented. The injuries — physical and psychological — reflect that failure.

Gunshot Wounds
Shootings in parking structures, nightclub lots, and apartment common areas cause soft tissue damage, fractures, organ injury, and permanent disability depending on the location and number of wounds — often requiring multiple surgeries and long-term rehabilitation.
Stab & Laceration Wounds
Robbery and assault attacks frequently involve edged weapons, producing deep lacerations, nerve damage, and internal injury when the wound penetrates the abdominal or chest cavity. Scarring and nerve damage often persist long after the wound itself heals.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Blows to the head during an assault, or a fall caused during a struggle or robbery, can cause concussion, diffuse axonal injury, or more severe TBI. Symptoms — memory loss, cognitive changes, headaches — sometimes emerge days after the attack.
Psychological Trauma & PTSD
Surviving a violent attack routinely produces post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, depression, and a lasting fear of public spaces or specific property types. Psychiatric evaluation and ongoing treatment are essential to documenting this real and compensable harm.
Fractures & Blunt Force Trauma
Beatings, robberies, and physical struggles during an assault produce facial fractures, broken ribs, and orthopedic injuries requiring surgical repair, hardware placement, and extended physical therapy to regain function.
Wrongful Death
When a preventable attack turns fatal, surviving family members can pursue a wrongful death claim against the property owner and operator for the security failures that allowed the attack to happen in the first place.
What they don't want you to know

The property's insurer has a playbook. So do we.

Property owners and their insurers carry a standard set of arguments in negligent security cases. Here's what they typically say — and how we dismantle each defense.

Tactic 01
"There's no history of crime at this property"
Owners routinely claim they had no way to know an attack was coming. Police CAD data and incident logs, obtained through a public records request, often reveal a documented pattern of 911 calls, prior robberies, and assaults the owner never disclosed — evidence that establishes exactly what a reasonable inspection of the property's crime history would have shown.
Tactic 02
"The criminal, not the property, is responsible"
This argument tries to end the case at the attacker, who is often unidentified or judgment-proof. California law imposes a separate duty on the property when the type of attack was foreseeable and reasonable security measures — working locks, functioning cameras, adequate guard staffing — would likely have prevented it.
Tactic 03
"We had reasonable security in place"
Insurers point to a security plan on paper while the actual conditions told a different story. Broken locks, disconnected cameras, and guard posts left unstaffed despite a known crime problem are specific, documentable failures — not judgment calls — and premises security experts translate them into a clear standard-of-care violation.
Tactic 04
"You assumed the risk by being there"
Insurers try to shift blame onto the victim for being in a parking structure at night or in a particular part of the property. California's pure comparative fault rule reduces recovery only by fault actually attributable to you — it does not eliminate the claim, and thorough documentation of the security failure minimizes any allocation of blame to you.

Questions & answers

Negligent security FAQ.

Negligent security is a premises liability claim against a property owner or operator whose failure to provide reasonable security measures allowed a third-party criminal act — an assault, shooting, or robbery — to occur on or near their property. Civil Code § 1714 requires owners and operators to exercise ordinary care in managing their property, and California courts have extended that duty to include reasonable measures protecting visitors from foreseeable criminal conduct by others.
California courts require a foreseeability analysis that weighs how predictable this type of crime was against the burden of providing additional security. Prior similar crimes on the property, in the immediate area, or logged in police call records are the strongest evidence of foreseeability. Courts have also found liability where the security failure itself was severe enough — a gate propped permanently open, a guard contract that lapsed and was never renewed — that even a thinner crime history was sufficient to put the owner on notice.
The most common failures are broken or malfunctioning locks and access gates, security cameras that were not operational or not monitored, no security guards on site despite a documented history of crime, and inadequate lighting in parking areas, stairwells, and entryways. We also examine whether the property ignored its own written security policies, understaffed a contracted guard service, or cut security measures to reduce costs.
Yes. Each property type carries different industry security standards and a different evidence trail. Nightclubs and bars are judged on guest capacity, bouncer staffing, and handling of visibly intoxicated patrons. Apartment complexes and hotels are judged on access control — working locks, gates, and key systems in common areas. Parking structures are judged on lighting, camera coverage, and guard patrols given their elevated crime risk. We tailor the security expert analysis to the specific property type involved.
Police computer-aided dispatch (CAD) records and incident logs for the property and surrounding block, obtained through a California Public Records Act request, typically provide the clearest picture of prior 911 calls, robberies, and assaults the owner never disclosed. We also pull prior lawsuits against the property, incident reports kept by on-site management or security contractors, and any security assessments or consultant recommendations the owner received and ignored.

Time limit

Two years to file. Camera footage disappears in days.

California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years from the date of the attack to file a personal injury lawsuit. But the evidence that wins negligent security cases — surveillance video, police CAD logs, prior incident reports, and security maintenance records — can be overwritten, lost, or destroyed if a preservation letter isn't sent immediately. If a government-owned property contributed to your attack, the deadline is six months under Government Code § 911.2. The sooner we start, the more evidence we lock down.
