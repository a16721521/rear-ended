Wide-turn truck collisions

A truck turns right. Everything on its right side gets crushed.

A wide-turn crash — also called a right-turn squeeze — happens when a large truck swings its cab left before executing a right turn. The maneuver is necessary because the trailer's rear axle tracks inside the cab's turning radius, and without the leftward swing the trailer would mount the curb. But that leftward swing opens a gap on the truck's right side that cyclists, motorcyclists, and smaller vehicles naturally enter — not realizing the truck is about to turn right across their path. The result is a compression collision between the trailer and the curb, with the smaller road user caught in between.

These crashes are preventable. Truck drivers holding a Commercial Driver's License are specifically trained to check right-side mirrors before and during any right turn. The "no-zone" — the blind spot along the truck's right side — is a core part of CDL education, and failing to clear it before turning is textbook negligence. Modern trucks increasingly carry convex mirrors, side-mounted cameras, and proximity sensors that eliminate the claimed blind spot entirely. When a driver turns right without confirming the right side is clear, the failure is not mechanical — it is human.

California Vehicle Code § 22100 requires right turns to be made as close to the right curb as practicable. Trucks get an exception — they may use more than one lane to complete the turn — but the exception does not waive their duty to yield to traffic lawfully occupying the right side. A cyclist in the bike lane or a vehicle alongside the truck has every legal right to be there. The truck's duty to check before turning is absolute.

Under California's pure comparative fault rule (Civil Code § 1431.2), even an allegation that the cyclist or driver "should have stayed back" only reduces recovery proportionally — it does not eliminate the claim. Wide-turn crashes produce catastrophic injuries and fatalities at rates far exceeding other truck collision types. Step-by-step

What to do after a wide-turn truck crash.

Wide-turn crashes happen at intersections — cameras are often nearby, and critical evidence disappears within hours. Acting fast preserves it.

1
At the scene
Call 911 and get a police report.

Get the responding officer's name, badge number, and report number. Wide-turn crashes at intersections often have witnesses. Ask officers to document the truck's position, any "wide turn" warning signage on the trailer, and whether the turn signal was activated.

2
At the scene
Photograph the truck's mirrors, cameras, and "wide turn" stickers.

Document the truck's right-side mirrors, any side-mounted camera systems, and warning signage on the trailer. Photograph the truck's final position relative to the curb and the crushed vehicle or bicycle. These details establish whether the driver had the equipment to check — and whether it was functioning.

3
Within hours
Identify intersection cameras and nearby surveillance.

Traffic signal cameras, business security systems, and doorbell cameras frequently capture intersection crashes. Most systems overwrite footage within 24 to 72 hours. Note every camera you see and contact us immediately so we can send preservation letters before the footage is gone.

4
Same day
Get medical attention, even if injuries seem minor.

Compression injuries from wide-turn crashes can involve internal damage that isn't immediately apparent. Pelvic fractures, internal bleeding, and crush injuries all require imaging to detect. A same-day medical visit creates the causation record your claim depends on.

5
Within 24 hours
Do not repair or discard the vehicle or bicycle.

The damage pattern on a crushed vehicle or bicycle documents the direction and force of compression. Tire marks on the vehicle's body can prove the trailer wheel rolled over it. Do not repair or discard anything until we've had it examined by a reconstruction expert.

What crush-zone impacts do

Wide-turn crashes compress.
The injuries reflect it.

A vehicle or cyclist caught between a turning trailer and the curb faces lateral compression forces that produce devastating, often fatal injuries.

Crush Injuries
The vehicle or cyclist is compressed between the trailer and the curb. Crush syndrome can cause organ failure even after extraction, requiring immediate trauma-center care and aggressive fluid resuscitation.
Amputations
Extremities caught between trailer wheels and pavement face traumatic amputation. Even when the limb is not severed on impact, the vascular damage from compression often makes surgical amputation necessary.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Cyclists and pedestrians struck by the turning trailer are thrown and strike the ground or surrounding objects with the head. Even helmeted cyclists sustain severe TBI at the force levels involved in these collisions.
Pelvic Fractures
Direct lateral compression from the trailer crushes the pelvis. These fractures carry significant internal bleeding risk, often require surgical fixation, and involve months of non-weight-bearing recovery.
Spinal Cord Injuries
Compression and rotational forces on the spine during a wide-turn crush can cause vertebral fractures and spinal cord damage, ranging from partial loss of function to complete paralysis below the injury level.
Fatal Injuries
Wide-turn crashes have among the highest fatality rates of any truck collision type. The combination of massive vehicle weight, compression forces, and the inability to escape the squeeze zone makes survival rates significantly lower than other crash configurations.
What they don't want you to know

The driver had every tool to prevent this. The insurer will pretend otherwise.

When a wide-turn crash happens, the trucking company's insurer pivots to shifting blame. Here's what they typically try.

Tactic 01
"The cyclist was in our blind spot"
The right-side blind spot is not a defense — it is the reason mirrors, convex lenses, and side cameras exist. CDL training specifically covers clearing the right-side "no-zone" before any right turn. If the driver couldn't see the right side, they had no business turning.
Tactic 02
"We had our turn signal on — they should have stayed back"
A turn signal does not transfer the duty to yield. The truck must ensure the right side is clear before and during the turn, regardless of signaling. A blinking light does not give a truck permission to crush the vehicle next to it.
Tactic 03
"The turn required a wide swing — that's standard procedure"
Wide swings are permitted under Vehicle Code § 22100, but the exception only allows using additional lanes — it does not waive the duty to yield to traffic lawfully on the right. The procedure includes checking. The driver skipped that step.
Tactic 04
"The other vehicle tried to pass on the right during the turn"
When a truck swings left before turning right, a reasonable driver or cyclist behind it may interpret the leftward movement as a lane change — not a setup for a right turn. The ambiguity the truck's maneuver creates is itself evidence the driver failed to communicate and clear the right side.
