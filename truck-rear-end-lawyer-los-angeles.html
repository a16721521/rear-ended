Truck rear-end highway crashes

20 times the mass. 40% longer to stop. Zero room for error.

A fully loaded commercial truck weighs up to 80,000 pounds — roughly 20 times the weight of the average passenger car. At 65 mph, that truck needs approximately 525 feet to come to a complete stop, compared to about 316 feet for a car. When a truck driver fails to maintain adequate following distance and rear-ends a vehicle at highway speed, the physics are unforgiving: the smaller vehicle absorbs nearly all of the impact energy while the truck barely decelerates. California Vehicle Code § 21703 requires every driver to maintain a safe following distance — and a professional CDL holder operating an 80,000-pound vehicle is held to a heightened standard.

These crashes are not random. They follow predictable patterns: fatigue from hours-of-service violations, distraction from phone use or in-cab devices, deferred brake maintenance that silently increases stopping distance, and overloading that pushes stopping requirements even further. Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) research confirms that following too closely and inadequate surveillance are among the top driver-related factors in large truck crashes.

The evidence in these cases is time-sensitive and carrier-controlled. The truck's electronic data recorder (EDR) captures pre-crash speed, brake application timing, throttle position, and following distance — but this data has limited retention, typically 30 to 180 days, and can be overwritten by subsequent driving events. Electronic logging device (ELD) records reveal whether the driver was compliant with hours-of-service rules or was driving fatigued. Dashcam footage, phone records, and carrier maintenance logs round out the picture. Without immediate legal intervention, carriers routinely allow this evidence to disappear.

California law creates a strong presumption that the rear-ending vehicle is at fault. Under Civil Code § 1431.2 (pure comparative fault), even if some fault is attributed to you, your recovery is reduced proportionally — not eliminated. What to do after a truck rear-end crash.

The truck's black box data and carrier records are the most valuable evidence in your case — and the carrier controls all of it. Acting fast is the only way to ensure it's preserved.

At the scene
Call 911 and request a commercial vehicle accident report.

Get the responding officer's name, badge number, and report number. Truck crashes involving injury trigger mandatory carrier reporting to the FMCSA. Note the trucking company name, DOT number, and trailer number — these are typically displayed on the cab and trailer.

At the scene
Photograph the truck, your vehicle, and the road.

Document the rear damage to your vehicle, the truck's front end, any skid marks or debris, the DOT and MC numbers on the cab, and the overall crash scene. If the truck has a dashcam visible, photograph it. Capture the road conditions, traffic density, and any posted speed limit signs.

Within hours
Call Law Dog to send a spoliation letter immediately.

The truck's black box data can be overwritten by subsequent driving events or routine maintenance. We send a formal evidence preservation letter to the carrier within hours of your call, legally obligating them to preserve the EDR data, ELD records, dashcam footage, driver qualification file, maintenance records, and dispatch communications.

Same day
Get emergency medical evaluation — even if you feel fine.

High-speed rear-end impacts subject your body to extreme acceleration forces. Spinal cord compression, traumatic brain injury from whiplash, and internal organ damage are not always immediately apparent. A same-day medical evaluation creates the causation link your claim depends on and catches injuries that worsen if untreated.

5
Within 48 hours
Do not give a recorded statement to any insurer.

The trucking company's insurer will contact you quickly — often the same day. Their adjuster is trained to elicit statements that can be used to shift blame (e.g., "Did you stop suddenly?"). Do not discuss the crash with anyone except your own attorney and medical providers.

6
Ongoing
Preserve your vehicle — do not repair or total it yet.

Your car's rear crush damage is physical evidence of the force of impact. The deformation pattern tells an accident reconstructionist exactly how fast the truck was traveling. Do not allow the insurance company to move, repair, or destroy your vehicle until our expert has examined it.

What rear-end truck impacts do

80,000 pounds of force
transferred into your vehicle.

When a commercial truck rear-ends a passenger car at highway speed, the occupants absorb forces far beyond what the vehicle was designed to withstand. The injury pattern reflects that disparity.

Spinal Cord Injuries
The massive rear compression force drives the seat back into the occupant's spine. Vertebral fractures, herniated discs, and in severe cases, partial or complete paralysis result from the sudden hyperextension and compression that a truck's mass inflicts on the spinal column.
Traumatic Brain Injury
Violent whiplash acceleration and deceleration causes the brain to impact the inside of the skull. Concussion, diffuse axonal injury, and subdural hematoma are common when the force levels exceed those of a typical car-on-car rear-end collision by an order of magnitude.
Crush Injuries
The vehicle's passenger compartment is compressed from behind, trapping occupants between the collapsing rear structure and the dashboard or steering column. Extrication by emergency crews is frequently required, and the resulting injuries often involve multiple body systems.
Internal Organ Damage
Seatbelt and steering wheel blunt trauma cause injuries to the liver, spleen, kidneys, and lungs. These internal injuries may not present symptoms for hours or days, making same-day imaging — CT scan and ultrasound — critical for early detection and treatment.
Cervical & Lumbar Disc Injuries
Whiplash at commercial vehicle force levels goes far beyond the typical fender-bender neck strain. Cervical and lumbar disc herniations, annular tears, and facet joint damage frequently require epidural injections, prolonged physical therapy, or surgical intervention including fusion.
Burns
Rear-end impact from a commercial truck can rupture the passenger vehicle's fuel tank or fuel lines, igniting a fire that traps occupants in the wreckage. Burn injuries from truck crashes are among the most severe, often requiring extensive skin grafting and reconstructive surgery.
What they don't want you to know

The rear vehicle is presumptively at fault. So they attack everything else.

When the basic liability picture is clear, trucking insurers pivot to manufactured defenses. Here's what to expect.

Tactic 01
"You stopped suddenly — you brake-checked the truck"
This is the most common defense in truck rear-end cases. It fails on the law: Vehicle Code § 21703 requires the following vehicle to maintain enough distance to stop safely regardless of what the vehicle ahead does. A professional CDL holder operating an 80,000-pound vehicle cannot tailgate and then blame the car in front for stopping.
Tactic 02
"Our driver was within safe following distance"
This claim is testable — and usually disproven by the truck's own black box data. The EDR records speed, brake application timing, and the gap between the initial brake input and the moment of impact. If the driver had been following at a safe distance, the crash would not have happened.
Tactic 03
"Your injuries are from a prior condition, not this crash"
Insurers review your medical history looking for any prior complaint — a previous back issue, an old neck strain — to argue the crash did not cause your current condition. California's eggshell plaintiff doctrine is clear: the defendant takes the plaintiff as they find them. If the crash aggravated a pre-existing condition, the defendant is liable for that aggravation.
Tactic 04
"Traffic was stop-and-go — everyone bears responsibility"
Congested highway conditions do not excuse following too closely. A professional CDL holder has a heightened duty of care in stop-and-go traffic precisely because the stopping distance of a loaded commercial vehicle makes tailgating in congestion extremely dangerous. The truck driver knew — or should have known — the risks of following too closely in heavy traffic.

Questions & answers

Truck rear-end crash FAQ.

A fully loaded commercial truck weighs up to 80,000 pounds — roughly 20 times the weight of a typical passenger car. The force differential is enormous: the truck barely decelerates while the smaller vehicle absorbs nearly all of the impact energy. The passenger compartment is compressed from behind, and occupants are subjected to violent acceleration forces. This mass-to-mass disparity is why truck rear-end crashes produce catastrophic injuries at rates far exceeding car-on-car collisions.
California law creates a strong presumption that the following vehicle — the rear-ending vehicle — is at fault. Vehicle Code § 21703 requires every driver to maintain a safe following distance, accounting for speed, traffic, and road conditions. A professional CDL holder operating an 80,000-pound vehicle is held to an even higher standard of care. The truck driver or carrier must demonstrate an extraordinary circumstance to overcome this presumption — and simple excuses like "traffic was heavy" rarely succeed.

The EDR (electronic data recorder) captures a snapshot of the truck's operating parameters in the seconds before and during the crash. This data reveals exactly how fast the truck was traveling, when the driver first applied the brakes, and the time gap between brake application and impact. Critically, this data has limited retention — typically 30 to 180 days — and can be overwritten by subsequent driving events.
Yes. The motor carrier is vicariously liable for its driver's on-duty negligence under respondeat superior. If the carrier's own conduct contributed to the crash — through inadequate maintenance, unrealistic scheduling that pressured the driver to violate hours-of-service rules, deficient training, or failure to enforce safety protocols — they face direct liability as well. Carrier liability often substantially increases the available insurance coverage and total recovery.
Comparative fault may apply, but a truck maintaining safe following distance should be able to avoid rear-ending a vehicle that changes lanes in front of it. The lane change does not eliminate the truck's duty to follow at a distance that allows safe stopping. Under California's pure comparative fault system (Civil Code § 1431.2), any fault attributed to you only reduces your recovery proportionally — it does not eliminate your claim.
