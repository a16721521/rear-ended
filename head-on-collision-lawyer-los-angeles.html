Head-on collisions

Opposite directions. Combined speed. Catastrophic result.

Head-on crashes are defined by physics that have no analog in other collision types. When two vehicles traveling in opposite directions collide, their speeds are additive — not separate. A 45 mph head-on equals the force of hitting a fixed wall at 90 mph, and the occupants of both vehicles absorb that combined energy. Survival rates that would be impossible in other scenarios are made possible only by modern vehicle safety engineering, and even so, the injuries that result are often the most severe personal injury law encounters.

The most common causes of head-on collisions in California include wrong-way drivers entering freeways at off-ramps, DUI impairment causing drivers to drift or turn into opposing traffic, passing maneuvers on two-lane highways that go tragically wrong, fatigued drivers crossing the center line on late-night corridors, and GPS errors that direct unfamiliar drivers the wrong way on one-way streets or freeway entrances. Each cause pattern leaves a distinct evidence signature — and each requires a different investigative approach.

The evidence that matters most in head-on crash cases has the shortest window: dashcam footage from either vehicle, toxicology results from blood draws performed at the scene or hospital, skid marks and yaw marks on the road surface, Event Data Recorder data from the vehicles' black boxes capturing speed and steering input in the seconds before impact, cell phone records showing distraction, and surveillance footage from businesses along the route the at-fault driver traveled before the collision. Each of these has a preservation deadline measured in hours or days, not weeks.

Many of our head-on collision clients are not themselves crash survivors — they are families of people who did not survive. Law Dog handles both survival injury claims and wrongful death actions arising from head-on crashes, often on behalf of the same family when multiple vehicle occupants were involved. If you lost someone in a head-on crash, the wrongful death and survival action claims are among the most significant in California law. Step-by-step

What to do after a head-on crash.

The actions taken in the first 72 hours after a head-on collision have an outsized effect on your recovery — both medical and legal. Follow these steps in order.

Free case review →
1
At the scene
Call 911 immediately.

Head-on crashes frequently involve serious injury to all parties. Get emergency services dispatched before anything else. Do not attempt to move anyone who may have spinal injuries unless there is an immediate fire or safety threat. The difference between moving and not moving a spinal cord injury victim can determine the difference between paralysis and recovery.

2
At the scene
Preserve the scene — do not move the vehicles.

If you can safely do so, preserve the scene. Do not move vehicles. Photograph both vehicles' final positions, the road markings (center line, passing zones, lane dividers), any skid marks, and the direction of travel. The point of rest tells accident reconstruction experts exactly what happened. Final vehicle positions in a head-on crash are among the most powerful physical evidence available.

3
Within hours
Request the toxicology results.

If the at-fault driver was impaired, law enforcement will typically draw blood at the scene or hospital. We obtain these results through discovery. If the driver fled or died, toxicology is the most powerful evidence of impairment — preserve all records. A confirmed DUI at the time of impact opens the door to punitive damages that no insurer can pay and that must come from the defendant personally.

4
Within 48 hours
Request the EDR data before the vehicle is repaired or totaled.

The Event Data Recorder (black box) captures speed, braking, throttle position, steering angle, and seatbelt status in the seconds before impact. Insurance companies move to preserve their insured's vehicle data — you should move faster with your attorney. Once the vehicle is released to a body shop or totaled, the data may be overwritten or inaccessible. We move immediately to secure it.

5
Within 48 hours
Identify all surveillance cameras near the crash site.

Gas stations, businesses, traffic cameras, and ATMs along the route the at-fault driver traveled may have captured wrong-way entry or impaired driving behavior miles before the collision point. Footage is overwritten in 24–72 hours. We subpoena it immediately — before the clock runs out. A wrong-way driver captured on freeway on-ramp surveillance miles before the crash is often the clearest evidence available.

6
Before you speak to anyone
Do not give a recorded statement to anyone.

The at-fault driver's insurer, your insurer, and even well-meaning first responders may ask questions. Your answers in the hours after a traumatic crash are often imprecise, inconsistent, and damaging to your claim. Let us handle all communication from the start. This is especially important in head-on crashes where the facts of which driver crossed which line may be disputed — your initial account becomes evidence, and it must be delivered strategically.

Common injuries

What the impact
actually does.

Head-on crashes produce the most severe injury profiles in personal injury law. We know which specialists to retain and how to document the full scope of lifetime damages.

Traumatic Brain Injury
The single most common catastrophic injury in head-on crashes. The brain decelerates into the skull at impact forces that can cause contusion, hemorrhage, diffuse axonal injury, and herniation. TBI from head-on crashes ranges from severe concussion to vegetative state. Neurological, cognitive, and behavioral changes may be permanent.
Spinal Cord Injury & Paralysis
The cervical spine is extremely vulnerable in head-on impacts. Complete or incomplete spinal cord injuries at C3–C7 produce paralysis, loss of sensation, and disruption of autonomic function. The lifetime care costs for a spinal cord injury — adaptive equipment, in-home attendant, housing modifications, lost earning capacity — routinely exceed $5 million.
Internal Organ Injuries
Seat belts and steering columns transmit tremendous force to the thoracic and abdominal cavities. Splenic laceration, liver trauma, aortic rupture, and pneumothorax are common in high-speed head-on crashes. Internal injuries frequently go undetected in initial trauma assessments — abdominal pain and distension in the hours after a crash require immediate re-evaluation.
Multiple Fractures
Lower extremity fractures from dashboard intrusion (femur, tibia, ankle), upper extremity fractures from bracing for impact, rib fractures, and pelvic fractures are characteristic of head-on crash injury patterns. Multiple fracture cases require orthopedic surgery, extended rehabilitation, and often result in chronic pain and limited mobility.
Facial & Maxillofacial Injuries
Airbag deployment and window glass at head-on impact speeds cause lacerations, orbital fractures, nasal fractures, dental avulsions, and jaw fractures. Reconstructive surgery, dental implants, and maxillofacial procedures are expensive and ongoing. Facial scarring from glass lacerations is a standalone damage category.
Wrongful Death
Head-on collisions are frequently fatal. If you lost a family member in a head-on crash, California law provides a wrongful death claim for the family and a survival action through the estate. These are often the highest-value personal injury cases California courts see. Law Dog handles wrongful death arising from head-on crashes with the same team that handles the survival claims.
What they don't want you to know

The playbook they run on head-on crash victims.

Insurance adjusters follow a script designed to minimize catastrophic injury payouts. Knowing what's coming is the first defense.

Tactic 01
Contributory Negligence Allegations
Even in clear wrong-way or DUI head-on cases, the defendant's insurer will look for any way to assign comparative fault to the victim: "Were you speeding?" "Did you take evasive action?" "Was your lighting working?" California's pure comparative fault system means any assigned percentage reduces your recovery — but does not bar it. We document your lawful conduct exhaustively to minimize any comparative fault allocation.
Tactic 02
Disputed Causation for Catastrophic Injuries
When injuries are severe, insurers frequently hire defense medical examiners (DMEs) to argue that the TBI or spinal injury was pre-existing, degenerative, or caused by a subsequent event rather than the crash. We retain top-tier neurologists, neurosurgeons, and spinal specialists to establish direct causation through imaging, clinical records, and accident reconstruction. Their hired expert faces our trial-tested specialists.
Tactic 03
Quick Settlement Before Treatment is Complete
Head-on crash victims are sometimes approached with settlement offers while still in the hospital. An offer that covers your initial hospitalization may be a fraction of your lifetime costs — particularly for TBI and spinal cord injuries where care needs evolve over years. We do not recommend any settlement until your treating physicians have established maximum medical improvement and a life care planner has projected future needs.
Tactic 04
Policy Limits Shell Game
At-fault drivers in DUI head-on crashes often carry minimum California liability coverage ($15,000/$30,000). When damages far exceed policy limits, we pursue: the at-fault driver personally, any commercial alcohol vendor under Dram Shop theories where applicable, the driver's employer if they were working, underinsured motorist coverage on your own policy, and umbrella policies. We find every dollar available.

Time limit

Two years. But black box data expires in days.

California Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1 gives you two years to file a personal injury lawsuit. But the practical urgency in head-on crashes is immediate: EDR black box data can be overwritten, surveillance footage disappears in 24–72 hours, toxicology samples degrade, and skid marks fade. If a government vehicle or road defect contributed, you have just six months under Government Code § 911.2. We move on day one. Evidence that exists today may not exist next week.
